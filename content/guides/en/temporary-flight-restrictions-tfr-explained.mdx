This guide covers the United States. A temporary flight restriction, or TFR, is airspace the FAA limits for a stated reason and a stated time, and it is published as a NOTAM rather than printed on a permanent chart. That is the feature that catches careful pilots: a check made the evening before an event can be out of date by the time the event starts.

## Key takeaways

- A TFR has its own NOTAM with its own area, altitude and times, so no single shape or distance fits all of them.
- Wildfire and other disaster TFRs are generally issued under 14 CFR 91.137. VIP restrictions come under 91.141, and major sporting events under 91.145.
- The FAA's standing notice for select sporting events covers a 3 nautical mile radius, up to and including 3,000 feet above ground level, from one hour before the scheduled start until one hour after the event ends, for stadiums seating 30,000 or more.
- Presidential TFRs have often been large; the FAA's pilot guide mentions radii of 30 nautical miles or more and about 10 nautical miles at Camp David.
- Always check the official FAA TFR list and a current NOTAM briefing right before launch.

## What a TFR is

A TFR restricts flight in a defined area for a defined period. The authority sits in several sections of 14 CFR Part 91, depending on the reason. Section 91.137 covers disaster and hazard areas and lets the FAA issue a NOTAM to protect people and property from a hazard associated with a surface incident, to provide a safe environment for disaster relief aircraft, or to prevent unsafe congestion of sightseeing and other aircraft above an incident or event of high public interest. The text does not name wildfires or set any radius; the NOTAM sets the area. Who may enter depends on the purpose of the TFR, and generally it is limited to aircraft taking part in the response, with a few listed exceptions that do not cover ordinary drone flights.

## Disaster and wildfire TFRs

The FAA's pilot guide says hazard TFRs under 91.137 keep non-participating traffic away from firefighting, avalanche control and search and rescue aircraft, and that they may last a few hours or several days. Their boundaries are not always circular, because they follow the needs of the agency coordinating the relief activities. The example NOTAM in the guide used a 10 nautical mile radius up to 8,000 feet MSL, but that is only one example. Our [wildfire guide](/en/guides/flying-near-wildfires-drone-restrictions) goes into the consequences for drone pilots.

## Stadiums and sporting events

Section 91.145 deals with aerial demonstrations and major sporting events. For a major sporting event the TFR normally covers a 3 nautical mile radius from the center of the event and up to 2,500 feet above the surface, and no more than necessary. The FAA does not issue separate TFRs for the regular events in its standing notice. That notice covers certain regular or postseason Major League Baseball, National Football League and NCAA Division I football games, plus certain NASCAR Cup, IndyCar and Champ Series races, at stadiums with a seating capacity of 30,000 or more. It applies within 3 nautical miles of the venue, up to and including 3,000 feet AGL, from one hour before the scheduled start until one hour after the event ends. The FAA says its Sporting Event Automated Monitoring System lists the affected events, locations and times. Knowing or willful violations can bring criminal penalties under 49 U.S.C. 46307, and a pilot may be stopped and questioned by law enforcement.

## VIP-movement TFRs

Section 91.141 covers flight restrictions in the proximity of the President and other parties. The FAA's pilot guide says these TFRs typically extend from the surface up to, but not including, flight level 180, that presidential TFRs with a 30 nautical mile radius or greater have been established in many cases, and that the restriction at Camp David is usually 10 nautical miles during presidential visits. The shape and the rules inside the area are set by each NOTAM, so read it. Do not plan a drone flight near a VIP movement without confirming the exact restriction with the FAA.

## Checking before you fly

The FAA advises checking the official TFR list at tfr.faa.gov and current NOTAMs. The guide also lists the Flight Service Station and the FAA website for graphic TFRs. Because TFRs can appear on short notice and times are given in UTC, check immediately before launch. Apps are useful for planning, but treat the official source as the one that decides. For the NOTAM system itself, see our [NOTAM guide](/en/guides/notam-explained), and for your wider airspace picture our [airspace classes guide](/en/guides/understanding-us-airspace-classes).

## Practical checklist

- Check tfr.faa.gov and a current NOTAM briefing immediately before launch, and again if your plans change.
- Near a stadium, assume the restriction runs from one hour before the start until one hour after the end of a covered event.
- Near visible smoke or an emergency response, assume restricted airspace even if no TFR has been posted yet.
- Do not plan any drone flight near a VIP movement without confirming the NOTAM.
- Read times in UTC carefully and convert them to local time.
- Confirm everything with the FAA. This site's airspace layers are planning references and do not show live TFR status.

## FAQ

### Is a TFR the same as a no-drone zone?

A TFR restricts the airspace for a purpose and a time, and for drones it works as a no-fly area unless you have the authorization the NOTAM describes. Read the NOTAM itself.

### How big is a stadium TFR?

Under the FAA's standing notice for select sporting events it is 3 nautical miles from the venue and up to and including 3,000 feet AGL, from one hour before the start to one hour after the end. Section 91.145 describes a normal 3 nautical mile radius up to 2,500 feet above the surface for TFRs it issues itself.

### Can I rely on an app?

Use an app to plan, then check the official FAA source. Apps can lag behind a changing situation.

### What happens if I fly in one?

The FAA lists possible criminal penalties for knowing or willful violations in the stadium notice, and drone pilots may face other charges under state and local law.

A quiet Kp reading at launch doesn't mean the same thing for a 4 km pipeline survey that it means for a five-minute hover over your backyard. Distance is exactly where geomagnetic disturbance is felt most: it can eat into GPS accuracy and return-to-home reliability, and those are the two systems a long-range mission depends on most.

## Key takeaways

- On long-range and BVLOS flights there is no visual backstop, so plan with a stricter Kp margin than for a short local flight.
- The thresholds below are conservative planning limits, not official rules. NOAA lists no satellite navigation effects at Kp 5–6 and intermittent problems from Kp 7.
- Kp is reported in 3-hour windows, so check the forecast for the whole mission window and re-check just before launch.
- NOAA's 3-day forecast is issued every 12 hours, and solar wind spacecraft at L1 typically give 15 to 60 minutes of lead time before a shock reaches Earth.
- Fix quality, satellite count and a sensible return margin matter as much as the Kp number.

## Why distance changes the risk math

On a short local flight, a pilot who notices the drone drifting in GPS mode can simply look up, switch to manual, and correct it in seconds. At 3-5 km out — or on any BVLOS mission where the drone is out of sight entirely — that option disappears. The pilot is flying on telemetry and a video feed that already has its own latency, and the only "eyes" the aircraft has are the same GPS and compass systems a geomagnetic storm can degrade. There is no visual backstop, so a small position error at launch becomes a real problem by the time it's noticed.

BVLOS flights also need the right authorization in most countries, and the rules differ by country. See [VLOS and BVLOS explained](/en/guides/vlos-bvlos-explained) and check your own aviation authority before planning one.

## Kp thresholds for long-range missions

- Kp 0-2: Ideal. Plan and fly long-range or mapping missions without extra restriction.
- Kp 3: Generally fine, but check the forecast trend, not just the current reading — a Kp of 3 that's forecast to climb during your flight window is a different situation than a stable Kp of 3.
- Kp 4: Acceptable for short-range or near-VLOS flights. For BVLOS or multi-kilometer missions, treat it as marginal — shorten the range or add a wider return-path buffer.
- Kp 5 or higher (G1+): Postpone BVLOS and long-range missions. This is the point where NOAA's storm scale begins, and it is a prudent place to stop for a mission that is farthest from home and least able to compensate manually.

These are this site's conservative planning limits. NOAA's scale page lists no satellite navigation effect at G1 or G2, intermittent navigation problems at G3 (Kp 7), and degradation for hours at G4 and possibly days at G5, so a Kp 5 or 6 mission is not guaranteed to go wrong. The margin is deliberate, because a long mission leaves little room to recover from a surprise.

## Kp is a flag, not a GPS meter

Kp measures magnetic disturbance at the ground. GPS errors come mainly from the ionosphere, and NOAA notes that single-frequency receivers, normally accurate to about a meter, can see errors of tens of meters or more in a severe storm. Because a Kp number is only an indirect indicator, also watch the satellite count and fix quality in your app, and remember that scintillation near the geomagnetic equator after sunset can affect GPS even when Kp is low. The guide on [how Kp affects GPS and compass reliability](/en/guides/kp-index-effect-on-gps-compass-reliability) explains the mechanism.

## A scenario: when the storm arrives mid-flight

The following is an illustrative example, not a documented case, and its numbers are invented to show how the planning problem works. A coastal mapping operator launches a 4.2 km grid survey at 9 a.m. with Kp reading 2 and no storm watch in the morning bulletin. By early afternoon, a coronal mass ejection that NOAA's forecast had flagged as a 40% chance arrives ahead of the wider window, and Kp climbs to 6 within a single 3-hour reporting period. The drone's automated RTH engages on schedule but tracks roughly 100 meters off the planned return line before the pilot notices the live telemetry map disagreeing with the expected path — enough to matter near the shoreline structures the survey was mapping in the first place. Nothing failed; the aircraft did what a degraded position solution can cause.

The real size of any error depends on latitude, time of day, receiver type and the strength of the ionospheric disturbance, and cannot be predicted from Kp alone. The lesson is the planning one: the conditions at 9 a.m. were not the conditions the mission flew through.

## The common mistake: checking Kp only once, at launch

Kp is reported in 3-hour windows, and a single pre-flight check only tells you the conditions for the window you launched in — not the two or three windows your mission will actually fly through. A multi-hour mapping or inspection flight planned the night before, with Kp checked only at the morning briefing, can fly straight into a storm that arrived in the meantime. Treat a single Kp check the same way you'd treat checking wind speed once and assuming it holds for a four-hour flight: technically compliant, but not actually safe.

## A better practice: watch the trend, not just the number

For any mission running longer than one Kp reporting window, pull NOAA's 3-day geomagnetic forecast before you commit to a launch time, and re-check the live value immediately before takeoff even if you reviewed the forecast the night before. Real-time solar wind data from the DSCOVR and ACE satellites, when available through your weather source, can give roughly 15-60 minutes of extra warning before a shock arrives at Earth, which is sometimes enough time to bring a long-range aircraft home, but it is not a guarantee and a coronal mass ejection can arrive hours to days after it is first seen.

## A planning timeline

| When | What to check | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Two to three days ahead | NOAA 3-day forecast, issued every 12 hours | Decide whether the mission window looks calm |
| The evening before | Updated forecast and any storm watch | Catch changes in timing |
| Morning of the flight | Live Kp and the trend | Confirm the forecast |
| Immediately before launch | Live Kp, solar wind data and fix quality at the site | Final go or no-go |
| In flight | Telemetry map, drift and satellite count | Detect trouble early and shorten the mission if needed |

## Practical checklist for long-range flights

- Check the full 3-day Kp/G-scale forecast for your entire mission window, not just the launch-time value.
- If any 3-hour segment during your planned flight is forecast at Kp 5 or higher, reschedule or shorten the mission range.
- Treat forecast Kp 4 as your ceiling for BVLOS or multi-kilometer missions, not Kp 5.
- Build in a return margin: define a distance or time threshold at which you trigger a manual RTH check rather than trusting the automated one blindly (see [return-to-home settings explained](/en/guides/return-to-home-settings-explained)).
- Re-check the live Kp reading immediately before launch, even for a mission planned and forecast-checked the night before.
- Check the [dashboard](/en) for the current Kp together with wind and other conditions.

## FAQ

### What is the best Kp for a long-range drone flight?

Kp 0–2 is ideal on this site's planning scale, and Kp 3 is generally fine if the forecast is stable. These are conservative planning limits rather than official rules.

### Is Kp 5 really unsafe for a long flight?

Not necessarily. NOAA lists no satellite navigation effects at G1 (Kp 5), but a long-range or BVLOS flight has little room to recover, so postponing is a prudent margin.

### How much warning do I get before a storm?

Spacecraft at the L1 point typically give 15 to 60 minutes of lead time before a shock reaches Earth, while coronal mass ejections themselves take roughly 15–18 hours at the fastest and several days when slower. The 3-day forecast gives a longer view.

### Should I trust return-to-home during a storm?

Treat it with caution, because it relies on the same GPS and compass data. Set a return margin and be ready to take manual control.

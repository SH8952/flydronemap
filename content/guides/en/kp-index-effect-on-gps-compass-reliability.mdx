Picture the moment: you are hovering at the edge of a field, the app says Kp 5, and the aircraft starts sliding sideways with the sticks centered. It is tempting to blame the storm and move on. Sometimes that is right. More often something closer to the ground is going on, and the useful skill is telling the two apart.

The short version is that a geomagnetic storm can reach your drone by two different routes, it rarely does so at the lower Kp levels, and the Kp number itself says nothing about which route is involved.

## Two instruments, two routes

A drone holds position with GPS and holds heading with a magnetometer. A storm has no single "effect on the drone"; it touches each of these in a different way.

| | GPS | Compass (magnetometer) |
|---|---|---|
| What it relies on | Radio signals crossing the ionosphere | The local magnetic field at the aircraft |
| How a storm gets in | The ionosphere is disturbed, so signals are delayed or flicker | The field the compass reads is itself pushed around |
| What NOAA and GFZ say | No navigation effect listed at G1 and G2 (Kp 5 and 6), intermittent problems at G3 (Kp 7), degraded for hours at G4 (Kp 8), possibly for days at G5 (Kp 9) | Strong storms can move a compass needle by a few degrees at high latitudes |
| Does Kp track it well? | Only loosely. Kp is measured on the ground, not in the ionosphere | Closer, since Kp is built from ground magnetic readings |

For the GPS route, the mechanism is covered in the guide on [ionospheric scintillation and drone GPS](/en/guides/ionospheric-scintillation-and-drone-gps), including why an equatorial evening can disturb a fix at a low Kp. NOAA's own summary is that single-frequency receivers, normally good to about a meter, can drift to tens of meters or more in a severe storm, and that dual-frequency receivers, normally good to centimeters, can fail outright when they lose lock. It also notes that major storms can strengthen ionospheric effects at mid-latitudes, not just near the poles. Which receiver type your drone uses is something only the manufacturer's specifications can tell you.

## What the compass actually sees

The Earth's field at the surface is somewhere between about 22,000 and 67,000 nT depending on where you stand. GFZ puts the outside contribution at up to roughly 100 nT in general and a few thousand nT during strong storms. Set against a field that large, a storm is a modest nudge, which fits with GFZ describing compass-needle changes of a few degrees, and only at high latitudes in strong storms.

How the flight controller reacts to a nudge like that depends on its firmware, and nothing public lets us say what any given model will do. Your manual is the reference there.

The practical point is the scale. Reinforced concrete, a parked car or a metal fence beside the takeoff spot are the usual local sources of magnetic interference, so a compass warning on a calm day points to the ground first and the sky second.

## Rule out the boring causes first

When something odd happens, work through these before you look at Kp:

1. **Compass calibration.** Was it done recently, and away from metal, vehicles and rebar? See [compass calibration and magnetic interference](/en/guides/compass-calibration-and-magnetic-interference).
2. **Satellite count and fix quality.** A weak fix near buildings or trees causes drift and circling that looks identical to a storm effect. See [GPS multipath error](/en/guides/gps-multipath-error-explained-for-drone-pilots).
3. **Deliberate interference.** Jamming and spoofing produce positioning trouble of their own. See [GPS jamming and spoofing](/en/guides/gps-jamming-and-spoofing-explained-for-drone-pilots).
4. **Then the storm.** If Kp is at storm level and the first three are clean, space weather becomes a reasonable suspect.

A quick way to read the situation:

| What you see | Kp is quiet | Kp is 5 or higher |
|---|---|---|
| Slow drift in GPS mode | Check satellites, surroundings, calibration | Still check those, then consider the storm |
| Compass warning that was absent yesterday at the same spot | Check for new metal nearby and recalibrate away from it | Storm is possible, though the sources only describe a few degrees at high latitudes |
| Return-to-home circling | Usually a fix or compass problem | Same, with extra reason to land manually |

## If you decide it probably is the storm

Take it as a reason to shorten the flight, not to push on. Keep the aircraft close and in sight, know how to drop into manual or attitude mode (your manual describes it), and land early if position hold does not settle. Return-to-home draws on the same GPS and compass data, so it is a poor escape hatch while those are in question.

Before the next flight, check the live Kp and NOAA's 3-day forecast on the [dashboard](/en), and wait at the site for a steady fix with a healthy satellite count. Do not rely on a single check at launch if you will be out for a while, since Kp is reported in 3-hour periods and can change in between.

## FAQ

### Does a low Kp mean my GPS is fine?

No. Kp describes disturbance measured on the ground. Scintillation near the geomagnetic equator can upset GPS after sunset whatever the Kp value is.

### Will my compass fail at Kp 5?

There is no official threshold that says so. The figure GFZ gives is a few degrees at high latitudes in strong storms, so local magnetic interference is the first thing to check.

### Is dual-frequency GPS immune to storms?

No. NOAA says dual-frequency receivers are normally accurate to centimeters but can become unreliable when they cannot lock onto the satellite signal.

### Should I land if the drone starts drifting?

If position hold does not recover after you switch to manual or attitude control as your manual describes, land early. Do not wait for return-to-home to fix it.

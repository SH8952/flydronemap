Most drone weather tools watch the Kp index, and Kp is built to describe geomagnetic storms. A solar flare is a different event on a different schedule. The radiation from a flare reaches Earth in about 8 minutes, and NOAA says its effect on the sunlit side of the upper atmosphere happens at the same time the flare is observed. So on a day when Kp reads a calm 2, the flare question can still be open, and the place to answer it is NOAA's X-ray flux plot, not the Kp gauge.

This guide is about that gap: what a flare does, how much of it we can actually attach to a drone, and how to tell a flare problem from a storm problem.

## Flares and storms run on different clocks

People say "solar storm" for two separate things. A flare is a burst of radiation, X-rays and extreme ultraviolet, and it arrives with the light. A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a cloud of charged plasma thrown off the Sun, and it takes much longer. NOAA gives as little as 15 to 18 hours for the fastest Earth-directed ones and several days for slower ones. CMEs are what drive geomagnetic storms, so they are what show up in Kp.

The two often travel together. NOAA notes that CMEs typically occur with flares, and NASA says the strongest flares are almost always correlated with CMEs. That is why a major flare is frequently followed a day or so later by a geomagnetic storm. It is also why a flare does not guarantee one: a flare can happen without an Earth-directed CME.

## What a flare does to the sky above you

NOAA's explanation is that the extra X-rays and extreme ultraviolet ionize the lower layers of the ionosphere on the sunlit side of Earth. The best-known result is a radio blackout, mainly in the 3 to 30 MHz HF band, which NASA says can last minutes or, in the worst cases, hours.

NOAA scales this by the flare's peak X-ray class. The length of the HF effect grows with the class: tens of minutes at R2 (M5), about an hour at R3 (X1), and one to two hours at R4 (X10). Low-frequency navigation signals follow a similar pattern, degraded for brief intervals at R1, tens of minutes at R2 and about an hour at R3. Increased satellite navigation errors only appear in the table at R5 (X20). The full table is in the guide to [NOAA's space weather scales](/en/guides/understanding-noaa-space-weather-scales).

One detail that is easy to miss: this only happens on the sunlit side. Whether a particular flight is exposed depends on where the Sun is over your location at that moment.

## What the GNSS research shows, and what it does not

Satellite navigation signals cross the ionosphere, so anything that changes its electron content changes their travel time. A study of the very active period of October 2014 measured typical midday total electron content of 30 to 40 TECU, with peaks above 50. On X-class flare days it was about 10 TECU higher than on the quiet days before. In the same period, coordinate deviations at three European stations were at least twice as large as in a calmer stretch, with vertical variations in precise point positioning running from millimeters to about 12 cm.

Those are results from fixed, high-precision stations. They are a good reason to be careful with centimeter-level work such as RTK or PPP mapping. They are not an error figure for a consumer drone's position hold, and none of the sources reviewed for this guide gives one. If someone quotes you a number for how many meters a flare will move a hobby drone, ask where it comes from.

## Radio bursts, briefly

Separately from the ionosphere, a flare can emit a burst of radio noise at the frequencies GPS uses. Australia's Bureau of Meteorology space weather service expects this only a few times per solar cycle, for a few minutes each time. It can reduce signal-to-noise ratio while a receiver is acquiring signals, though an established lock is rarely lost. It does describe one 2006 event that caused loss of lock with GPS satellites for around two hours at a single observatory. For how solar activity relates to the control link itself, see [solar wind and drone communication interference](/en/guides/solar-wind-and-drone-communication-interference).

## Flare or storm: reading the clues

If your GPS misbehaves and you want to know which of the two you are dealing with, the timing is the best clue.

| Clue | Flare-driven | Storm-driven |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Starts when the flare peaks, within minutes | Builds over hours, often a day or more after the eruption |
| Kp | Often still low | Rising; 5 or more is a storm |
| Where | Sunlit side | Strongest at high latitudes, reaching lower latitudes in major storms |
| What to open | GOES X-ray flux, R-scale alerts | Kp, G-scale, aurora forecasts |
| How long | Tens of minutes to a few hours | Hours or longer |

For the storm side at high latitude, see [aurora season and high-latitude navigation anomalies](/en/guides/aurora-season-and-high-latitude-drone-navigation-anomalies).

## What to check before a precision flight

NOAA's GOES X-ray plots carry 1-minute averages in two passbands and update every minute, and NOAA sends alerts when a flare reaches M5. The 3-day forecast, issued every 12 hours, breaks out forecast and observed activity for each of the three scale categories. Because a flare's effect arrives as it is observed, the live plot is the useful preflight check, and the forecast is for planning the days ahead.

Keep it in proportion, too. NOAA's table counts roughly 2,000 R1 events per solar cycle, about 175 at R3 and about 8 at R4. A flare day is not automatically a dangerous flight day, and the sources do not quantify drone-level effects.

For ordinary flying, a glance at the X-ray plot next to Kp is enough. If GPS does turn unstable in the middle of a precise mission and a recent flare is on the plot, count it as one possible cause alongside the usual ones, wait it out as the documented HF effects last tens of minutes to a couple of hours, and keep a manual fallback ready, as described in [what to do when drone GPS signal is weak](/en/guides/what-to-do-when-drone-gps-signal-is-weak). The [FlyDroneMap dashboard](/en) covers Kp but does not replace the X-ray plot.

## FAQ

### Does a solar flare jam my drone's radio link?

The documented flare effects are on HF radio, on low-frequency navigation and, in research, on GNSS accuracy. None of the sources reviewed here describes flares jamming 2.4 GHz or 5.8 GHz control links.

### Why is Kp calm during a flare?

Kp measures geomagnetic disturbance, which depends on charged particles and magnetic fields arriving from the Sun. A flare's radiation arrives in minutes, while a CME takes at least about 15 hours.

### Do I need to stop flying every time there is a flare?

The sources give no rule for drones. Look at the flare class and at how your aircraft's positioning is behaving, then decide based on your mission and local regulations.

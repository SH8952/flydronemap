Imagine a precision mapping flight where GPS position hold degrades for a while even though the Kp index still reads a calm 2. One thing worth checking in that situation is NOAA's X-ray flux plot. A strong solar flare acts on the sunlit side of Earth's atmosphere within minutes, which is a different clock from the slower geomagnetic storms that most drone weather tools are built to track.

## Key takeaways

- A solar flare's radiation reaches Earth in about 8 minutes, and NOAA says its effect on the sunlit side of the upper atmosphere occurs at the same time the event is observed.
- The effects that NOAA documents for flares are HF radio blackouts (3 to 30 MHz) and degraded low-frequency navigation signals. NOAA's R-scale mentions increased satellite navigation errors only at R5.
- Research on strong flares has measured increased ionospheric electron content and larger errors in precise GNSS positioning. No source reviewed here quantifies the effect on a consumer drone's position hold.
- Rare solar radio bursts can also disturb GPS reception for minutes.
- Kp tells you about geomagnetic storms, not flares. Live X-ray flux is the relevant data for flares.

## Flares and CMEs are not the same thing

People often use "solar storm" for both, but they behave differently.

| | Solar flare | Coronal mass ejection (CME) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A burst of radiation (X-rays and extreme ultraviolet) | A cloud of charged plasma thrown out from the Sun |
| Time to reach Earth | About 8 minutes, the travel time of light | NOAA: as little as 15 to 18 hours for the fastest Earth-directed ones, several days for slower ones |
| Where it acts | The sunlit side of Earth's atmosphere | Earth's magnetic environment and ionosphere, with the strongest effects at high latitudes |
| Related scale | R (Radio Blackouts) | G (Geomagnetic Storms), through Kp |
| Duration | Minutes to hours | A resulting storm can last hours or longer |

NOAA notes that CMEs typically occur together with flares, and NASA says the strongest flares are almost always correlated with CMEs. That is why a major flare is often followed by a geomagnetic storm a day or more later, but a flare can also happen without an Earth-directed CME.

## How a flare disturbs the ionosphere

NOAA explains that the increased X-ray and extreme ultraviolet radiation from a flare ionizes the lower layers of the ionosphere on the sunlit side of Earth. That produces a radio blackout: the absence of HF communication, primarily in the 3 to 30 MHz band. NASA describes the result as radio signal blackouts that can last for minutes or, in the worst cases, hours.

NOAA's R-scale ties severity to the flare's peak X-ray class, and the duration of HF effects grows with the class: tens of minutes at R2 (M5), about an hour at R3 (X1), and one to two hours at R4 (X10). The same table lists low-frequency navigation signals as degraded for brief intervals at R1, tens of minutes at R2, about an hour at R3, and mentions increased satellite navigation errors only at R5 (X20). See [the NOAA scales explained](/en/guides/understanding-noaa-space-weather-scales) for the full table.

Only the sunlit hemisphere is exposed to this effect. Whether a given flight is affected therefore depends on where the Sun is relative to the flight location at the time.

## What research says about GNSS

Satellite navigation signals pass through the ionosphere, and anything that changes its electron content changes the signal's travel time. A study of the very active period of October 2014 measured typical midday total electron content of 30 to 40 TECU, with peaks above 50 TECU, and about 10 TECU of additional increase during X-class flare days compared with preceding quiet days. In the same period, coordinate deviations at three European stations were at least twice as large as in a quieter period. The reported vertical variations in precise point positioning were in the range of millimeters to about 12 cm.

These figures come from high-precision positioning at fixed stations. They show that flares and active periods can matter for centimeter-level work such as RTK or PPP mapping. They do not translate directly into an error number for a consumer drone's position hold, and no source reviewed for this guide gives one.

## Solar radio bursts

Separately from the ionospheric effect, a flare can produce a burst of radio noise at the frequencies GPS uses. The Australian Bureau of Meteorology's space weather service says such interference is expected only a few times per solar cycle and only for a few minutes each time. It can reduce signal-to-noise ratio during signal acquisition, though an established lock is rarely lost, and it describes a 2006 event that caused loss of lock with GPS satellites for around two hours at one observatory. This is rare, and it is not a reason to expect trouble on every flare day.

## Flare or geomagnetic storm: how to tell

| Clue | Flare-driven | Storm-driven |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Starts when the flare peaks, within minutes | Builds over hours, often a day or more after the eruption |
| Kp | Often still low | Rising, 5 or more indicates a storm |
| Location | Sunlit side | Strongest at high latitudes, can reach lower latitudes in major storms |
| Where to look | GOES X-ray flux, R-scale alerts | Kp, G-scale, aurora forecasts |
| Typical length | Tens of minutes to a few hours | Hours or longer |

For storms and their effects at high latitude, see [aurora season and high-latitude navigation anomalies](/en/guides/aurora-season-and-high-latitude-drone-navigation-anomalies). The related guide on ionospheric scintillation covers that mechanism in more depth.

## What to watch before and during a flight

NOAA's GOES X-ray plots contain 1-minute averages in two passbands and update every minute. NOAA sends space weather alerts at the M5 level. The 3-day forecast is issued every 12 hours, with forecast and observed information broken down for each of the three scale categories. Because a flare's effect occurs at the same time it is observed, the live plot is the useful preflight check; a forecast helps with the days ahead.

## Common mistakes

- Using Kp as a complete space weather check.
- Blaming a radio link or a drone fault without considering other causes. Many things degrade positioning: obstructions, multipath, a low satellite count, and local interference.
- Assuming any flare day means a dangerous flight. NOAA's table counts roughly 2,000 R1 events per solar cycle but only about 175 at R3 and about 8 at R4, and the available sources do not quantify drone-level effects.
- Forgetting that the effect is on the sunlit side only.

## Checklist

- Before precision work, look at the live GOES X-ray flux and any active R-scale alert, in addition to Kp.
- If GPS becomes unstable during a precise mission, consider a recent flare as one possible cause, along with the usual ones.
- If you suspect a flare effect, wait and observe. Documented HF effects last from tens of minutes to a couple of hours, depending on the level.
- Keep a manual fallback ready. See [what to do when drone GPS signal is weak](/en/guides/what-to-do-when-drone-gps-signal-is-weak).
- Use the [FlyDroneMap dashboard](/en) for Kp, but remember that it does not replace the X-ray plot for flares.

## FAQ

### Does a solar flare jam my drone's radio link?

The documented flare effects are on HF radio, low-frequency navigation, and, in research, on GNSS accuracy. None of the sources reviewed for this guide describes flares jamming 2.4 GHz or 5.8 GHz control links.

### How quickly does a flare affect the atmosphere?

NOAA says the effect on the sunlit side happens at the same time the event is observed. NASA gives the light travel time from the Sun as about 8 minutes.

### Why is Kp calm during a flare?

Kp measures geomagnetic disturbance, which depends on charged particles and magnetic fields arriving from the Sun. A flare's radiation arrives in minutes, while a CME takes at least about 15 hours.

### Do I need to stop flying every time there is a flare?

The sources do not give a rule for drones. Check the flare class and the status of your aircraft's positioning, and decide based on your mission and local regulations.

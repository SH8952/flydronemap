If your drone suddenly loses GPS lock near an airport, a military installation, or a site with heightened security, it is natural to suspect a satellite or software glitch. Sometimes the cause is deliberate interference. GPS jamming and spoofing are aimed at receivers on purpose, unlike a Kp-index spike or a multipath reflection off a nearby building, which means the failure mode is different and so is the right response.

## Key takeaways

- Jamming denies the signal: the receiver cannot acquire or track satellites. Spoofing fakes it: counterfeit GNSS-like signals can make a receiver compute a wrong position.
- The FAA's resource guide lists jamming indicators such as GNSS failure messages, and spoofing indicators such as disagreement between navigation systems, abnormal ground speed and sudden position shifts.
- EASA says spoofing carries more safety risk than jamming because it takes longer to recognise. It names areas around conflict or geopolitical risk zones, including parts of the Mediterranean, Black Sea, Middle East, Baltic Sea and Arctic, as affected.
- Return-to-Home depends on GNSS position. If you suspect interference, fly by sight and land manually instead of relying on automation.
- Using a GPS jammer is prohibited under US federal law, according to GPS.gov. Rules in other countries differ.

## Jamming denies the signal, spoofing fakes it

The FAA's GNSS Interference Resource Guide, written for aviation, defines jamming as emissions that do not mimic GNSS signals but interfere with the receiver's ability to acquire and track them. The result is signal denial. Spoofing is the emission of GNSS-like signals that a receiver may track in combination with, or instead of, the real ones, potentially giving false position and timing data. EASA uses similar definitions in its Safety Information Bulletin on GNSS outages and alterations and adds that spoofing carries more safety risk than jamming because crews recognise it later.

Those documents were written for crewed aircraft, but the physics is the same for a drone's receiver. How a particular drone reacts depends on the manufacturer and model, so check your manual for what the aircraft does when satellite positioning is lost.

## How this differs from space weather and multipath

| Cause | Scale | Timing | What you may see |
|---|---|---|---|
| Space weather | Wide region | Tied to a storm window or, near the equator, local evening | Degraded accuracy or lock loss for many users |
| Multipath | One spot near reflectors | Any day | Repeatable drift at the same place |
| Jamming | Around a transmitter | Can appear and vanish abruptly | GPS lost, warnings on the display |
| Spoofing | Around a transmitter | Can appear and vanish abruptly | Position that looks valid but is wrong or inconsistent |

The pattern to watch for is abruptness and mismatch. Space-weather degradation is broad and tied to a storm window, as described in [how the Kp index affects GPS and compass reliability](/en/guides/kp-index-effect-on-gps-compass-reliability). Multipath is fixed to a physical spot, as in [GPS multipath error explained](/en/guides/gps-multipath-error-explained-for-drone-pilots). Interference by a transmitter is local to that transmitter and is not linked to space-weather data.

## What the aviation guidance says to look for

The FAA guide separates the signs. For jamming it lists GNSS failure messages on flight displays and loss of ADS-B and data-link functions. For spoofing, which it calls more insidious, it lists position shifts of several miles in seconds, disagreement between navigation systems, abnormal ground speed and wind indications, clock time changes and sudden autopilot course deviations. A small drone has fewer instruments to compare, so the same idea translates into a simple habit: compare what the app says with what you can see.

- Does the aircraft marker on the map match where the drone is in the sky?
- Does the drone respond to stick inputs the way it normally would, or does it drift in an unexpected direction while the app claims a strong lock?
- Did the home point or the map position jump suddenly?
- Do other devices nearby, such as a phone, show odd positions as well?

## Where interference has been reported

EASA's bulletin names areas surrounding conflict or geopolitical risk zones, citing the south and eastern Mediterranean, the Black Sea, the Middle East, the Baltic Sea and the Arctic, and it points to its website for the authoritative list of affected flight information regions. The list changes, so use the current official one rather than a fixed memory of it. For drone flights, the practical use of this information is planning: if you travel to such a region, expect the possibility of degraded GNSS and check the local rules and notices before flying.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting the displayed GPS status over the behavior of the aircraft. A fake signal can look as healthy as a real one.
- Relying on Return-to-Home when interference is suspected. DJI's Return-to-Home documentation lists a strong GPS signal and a successfully recorded home point as preconditions for failsafe RTH, so a suspect position makes automatic return unreliable. This varies by manufacturer and model.
- Assuming a calm Kp index means GPS is safe. Interference has nothing to do with space weather.
- Ignoring written notices. The FAA guide recommends checking NOTAMs for interference areas in preflight planning.

## What to do if you suspect jamming or spoofing

- If lock drops suddenly with no gradual degradation beforehand, stay calm, keep the drone in sight and switch to a manual or attitude-type mode if your aircraft offers one. See [what to do when drone GPS signal is weak](/en/guides/what-to-do-when-drone-gps-signal-is-weak) for the flight sequence.
- If what you see disagrees with the position on screen, trust your eyes, fly the aircraft by hand and land at the nearest safe spot.
- Before flights near airports or other sensitive sites, check published notices; see the guide to [NOTAMs](/en/guides/notam-explained).
- Keep visual positioning or another secondary reference enabled if your drone has one, as an extra cross-check.
- After landing, note the place and time. The FAA guide asks pilots of aircraft to report anomalies, and GPS.gov says that in the US suspected jamming can be reported through a complaint to the FCC. Ask your local authority how drone pilots should report interference.

## FAQ

### Is GPS jamming legal?

In the United States, GPS.gov states that federal law prohibits operating, marketing or selling jamming equipment that interferes with authorized radio communications, including GPS. Other countries have their own rules, so check with your national regulator.

### Can I tell spoofing from a normal GPS error?

Not from the satellite count alone. Look for mismatches: the aircraft behaves differently from what the display claims, the map position jumps, or the marker disagrees with what you see in the sky.

### Will Return-to-Home save me?

Not reliably. RTH depends on GNSS position, and DJI's manual for the Mini 4K says the aircraft cannot return if the GPS signal is weak or unavailable. Behavior differs by model, so read your own manual.

### Is interference common near airports?

It depends on the location. EASA and the FAA both publish guidance because interference is a recognised problem for aircraft, and the FAA advises checking NOTAMs for interference areas. Check notices for your area before flying.

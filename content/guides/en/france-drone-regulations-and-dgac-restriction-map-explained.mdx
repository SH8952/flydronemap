Pilots who know the EU open category often assume the rules are identical in every member state. France shows why that is risky. The DGAC, the French civil aviation authority, applies EU Implementing Regulation 2019/947 and adds national rules on top, and it says itself that national specifics should be checked in each member state. This guide separates the two layers: what comes from the EU framework, and what France adds, including the DGAC restriction map and the limits the DGAC states for that map.

## Registration depends on weight or sensors, and 800 g is a different threshold

According to the DGAC, an operator must register as a European UAS operator before any open-category activity if the drone weighs more than 250 grams, or if it is not a toy and carries sensors that can collect personal data, such as a camera or a microphone. Registration is done online through the AlphaTango portal. Once that is done, the DGAC says open-category flying itself needs no declaration and no prior authorisation, but it must comply with the European regulations and the national orders.

The 800 g figure appears elsewhere on the DGAC page and is a separate matter. Under French rules, drones heavier than 800 g must emit an electronic signal, with some stated exemptions, for example for members of certain model-aircraft associations flying at designated sites. The DGAC's description of penalties for missing training or registration also refers to drones of 800 g or more. Treat 250 g as the registration trigger and 800 g as an additional French threshold, and read the DGAC page for how each applies to your drone.

## Training depends on the subcategory, and the minimum age is 14

The DGAC gives 14 as the minimum age for flying in the open category in France and notes that other member states can set a higher age. For subcategories A1/A3 the pilot must complete training and pass an exam of 40 questions, with 75 percent correct answers expected. The DGAC says the certificate is recognised in all EU member states and that the training and exam are free and can be taken online, including those offered by any other member state.

For A2, practical training follows the A1/A3 theory. It can be self-training under A3 conditions, meaning far from people and at least 150 m from residential, recreational, industrial or commercial areas. A further exam of thirty questions follows, again with 75 percent expected, covering meteorology, UAS flight performance and technical and operational ground-risk mitigation. In France the A2 exam can be taken online from the AlphaTango account or at an exam centre.

One older document matters if you hold it. The DGAC states that BAPD certificates obtained by sworn declaration are no longer valid since 1 January 2026, and it describes the steps for holders who want to renew. The page also notes that the DGAC's own training and exam are still being finalised, so check the DGAC training page for what is available now.

## A1, A2 and A3 set distances from people, and flying over crowds is never allowed

The DGAC's overview table follows the EU structure. A1 covers class C0 and C1 drones: overflight of people is tolerated with C0 (under 250 g) and, with C1, allowed only if accidental. A2 covers class C2: overflight is prohibited, and the distance to people is 5 m in low-speed mode and 30 m otherwise. A3 covers C2, C3 and C4: overflight is prohibited in all cases, and the table gives 150 m from people. In its training text the DGAC describes A3 conditions as at least 150 m from residential, recreational, industrial or commercial areas, so use the DGAC open-category guide for the exact wording.

Drones without a European class marking that were already on the market may be used under A1 conditions if they weigh under 250 g, and under A3 conditions if they weigh over 250 g. Flying over gatherings of people is never allowed in the open category.

## France adds national limits: no night flights and no open-category flights in public spaces in built-up areas

The DGAC reminds pilots that in the open category night flight is prohibited, flight in public space in built-up areas is prohibited, and the maximum height is 120 m. The prefect can grant exceptions to the night ban, and the DGAC says the request form must be sent at least 30 days before the flights. For visitors the DGAC adds that a flight in public space in a built-up area is then not open category but falls under the Specific category.

The framework text is the order of 3 December 2020 on the use of airspace by unmanned aircraft. The DGAC says it replaced the order of 17 December 2015 and takes into account the lowering of the maximum height from 150 m to 120 m. The DGAC page lists the order together with a modification dated 23 December 2025, so check the consolidated text on Légifrance, which the DGAC page links to.

## The DGAC restriction map is a useful tool with stated gaps

With the IGN, the DGAC built an interactive map of restrictions for leisure drones. According to the DGAC it shows, in a simplified way, zones between 0 and 120 m across metropolitan France where flights are prohibited or restricted, based on the order of 3 December 2020. In restricted zones flights are either entirely prohibited or need a specific authorisation from the zone manager, so open-category flying without authorisation is not possible there. The map legend uses categories such as flight prohibited, maximum heights of 30, 50, 60 or 100 m and a notification category for aircraft over 900 g, and it notes that particular conditions in the order may allow exceptions.

The DGAC is explicit that the map complements knowledge of the rules and does not replace it. It lists limits: private helipads are not shown, temporary overflight bans in natural areas during nesting periods are not shown, and for temporary zones in general you must check the website of the Aeronautical Information Service (SIA). A separate catalogue of geographical zones in the EUROCAE ED-269 format is published on the SIA site. FlyDroneMap's [home map](/en) also displays this layer for reference, but confirm on the official map and with the SIA before a flight.

## Privacy and no-capture zones are separate from airspace rules

The DGAC states that people's privacy must be respected. People present should at least be told if the drone carries a camera or another sensor, and publishing images that identify people needs their consent, or that of the owner in a private space. The zones where capturing aerial data is prohibited are listed in the order of 2 January 2023, and the DGAC notes that the obligation to declare aerial photography has been abolished. The DGAC also lists penalties up to imprisonment and heavy fines for flying under non-compliant conditions, flying over a prohibited zone or breaching privacy. This guide gives no amounts, so look them up on the DGAC page and the decrees it links to.

## Visitors need EU registration, and the DGAC names some French extras

For operators from another EU member state, the DGAC says the operator must be registered as a European operator, meet all open-category requirements and take into account restrictions set by France. It then names French specifics: all drones over 800 g must be registered on AlphaTango and carry a French electronic signalling device, the open-category height limits at the planned location are given on the DGAC restriction sheet, and open-category flights in public space in built-up areas are prohibited.

For operators from a state that does not apply the EASA regulations, the DGAC says to register as a European operator in the first EU country where the operation is planned, and that there is no equivalence or recognition of remote pilot titles: the European qualifications for the open category are required. Read the DGAC page for how this applies to your visit.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Assuming that 250 g and 800 g are the same threshold, or that a camera drone under 800 g needs no registration.
- Treating the EU open category as permission to fly anywhere, and overlooking the national bans on night flights and on open-category flights in public spaces in built-up areas.
- Treating the restriction map as complete, although the DGAC itself lists private helipads, nesting-period bans and temporary zones as gaps.
- Relying on outdated information, such as the 150 m height limit from the 2015 order or a BAPD obtained by sworn declaration.

## Practical checklist

- Register as a UAS operator on AlphaTango before flying if the drone is over 250 g, or is not a toy and carries a camera or microphone.
- Make sure you hold the right certificate (A1/A3, or A2 when you fly C2 drones at the A2 distances) and meet the minimum age of 14.
- Keep your registration and training documents at hand, because the DGAC describes pilots having to present them in checks.
- Before every flight, look at the DGAC restriction map for the exact location and check the SIA for temporary zones, remembering what the map does not show.
- Do not plan night flights, flights in public space in built-up areas or flights above 120 m in the open category.
- Always check current requirements through the official channels of the DGAC. This guide is orientation only and does not replace a flight permit or legal advice.

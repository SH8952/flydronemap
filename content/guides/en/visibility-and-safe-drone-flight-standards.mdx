Visual line of sight (VLOS) is a foundational requirement in drone rules such as the FAA's Part 107, and visibility distance is what determines whether it is actually achievable.

## Key takeaways

- In the United States, 14 CFR 107.51 requires at least 3 statute miles (about 4.8 km) of flight visibility, observed from the control station, plus 500 feet below and 2,000 feet horizontally from clouds.
- The Japan Meteorological Agency defines fog as visibility under 1 km, and the US National Weather Service calls fog often hazardous at one quarter mile or less, so fog is far below the US drone minimum.
- Mist and haze can fail the minimum too: in aviation reports, mist covers 5/8 to 6 statute miles, and haze is reported when visibility is under 7 miles.
- Other countries word their rules differently, so check the regulator for the country you fly in.
- Check the current visibility, not only cloud cover, and bring the aircraft back well before you would lose sight of it.

## What "visibility" means for flying

The NWS glossary defines visibility as the distance at which a given standard object can be seen and identified with the unaided eye. In practice, haze, fog, smoke, heavy rain, and even bright low-sun glare can all reduce it well below what a forecast's "clear" label implies.

Visibility can also differ by direction. JMA notes that when it varies with direction, visibility generally means the shortest distance. Looking toward a bright, hazy sun can be much worse than looking away from it, so judge it in the direction you will actually fly.

## What regulations say

United States. 14 CFR 107.51 states that the minimum flight visibility, as observed from the location of the control station, must be no less than 3 statute miles, and the aircraft must remain no less than 500 feet below a cloud and 2,000 feet horizontally from it. Separately, 107.31 requires that the remote pilot in command and the person manipulating the controls, or a visual observer, can see the aircraft throughout the entire flight, without aids other than corrective lenses. See the [US regulations page](/en/regulations/us) and the guide to VLOS and BVLOS.

Other countries. Rules differ by country and change over time. Where a regulator gives no visibility figure, the practical test is still the same: can you see the aircraft and the airspace around it? Use the regulator's official pages for your location and treat this guide as general information, not legal advice.

## How weather reports classify reduced visibility

| Term | What the source says |
|---|---|
| Fog (NWS glossary) | Water droplets suspended in the air at the surface; often hazardous when visibility is reduced to 1/4 mile or less |
| Fog (JMA) | Visibility under 1 km; dense fog is about 100 m or less over land |
| Mist (aviation report) | Visibility of at least 5/8 and not more than 6 statute miles |
| Haze (aviation report) | Reported only when visibility is under 7 miles or operationally significant |

A practical lesson is that a report of mist or haze, not only fog, is a reason to look more carefully at whether your 3 statute miles are really there.

## Why this matters beyond compliance

Reduced visibility does not just risk a violation. It directly increases collision risk with manned aircraft, especially near airports, and makes it easy to lose orientation of a small drone against a hazy sky. It also interacts with other hazards: [fog](/en/guides/flying-in-fog-safety-guide) puts moisture on the aircraft and degrades obstacle sensors, and rain lowers visibility while wetting electronics, as covered in the guide on why rain is dangerous.

## Practical checklist

- Look at the current visibility for your location, from an observation or by eye toward the horizon, not just a cloud-cover percentage.
- Judge it in the direction of flight, and consider low sun and glare.
- Remember that US rules also set cloud clearances, so look at cloud base as well as visibility.
- Expect visibility to change at dawn, dusk and when fog is forming or lifting.
- Keep the flight short and close enough that you can see the orientation of the aircraft, not just a dot.
- Bring the drone back well before you would actually lose sight of it.

## Common mistakes

- Judging from indoors. A day can look clear while haze near the horizon limits practical VLOS.
- Trusting the sky colour. Blue sky overhead does not mean a long horizontal range.
- Relying on the video feed. The rules above are about seeing the aircraft, not the screen.
- Waiting too long. By the time the aircraft is a faint speck, you may already be out of compliance.

## FAQ

### What is the minimum visibility for flying a drone?

In the United States, 14 CFR 107.51 sets 3 statute miles of flight visibility as observed from the control station. Other countries use different wording, so check your own regulator.

### Is visibility the same as cloud cover?

No. Cloud cover describes how much of the sky is covered, while visibility is how far you can see and identify objects. Haze can limit visibility on a day with few clouds.

### Can I fly in mist or haze?

It depends on how far you can actually see and on your local rules. In aviation reports, mist means visibility between 5/8 and 6 statute miles, which may be below the 3 statute mile US minimum at the low end, so check the reported figure.

### Does a camera view count as line of sight?

Under the US rules cited here, the remote pilot and the person manipulating the controls, or a visual observer, must be able to see the aircraft; the rule describes unaided vision apart from corrective lenses.

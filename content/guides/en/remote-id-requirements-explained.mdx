This guide covers the United States. Remote ID is an FAA rule, and it applies to the drone you fly on the day you fly it, not to the day you bought it. A pilot who dusts off a registered drone from 2021 and flies it in a park today is held to the current rule, whatever the box said at the time of purchase.

## Key takeaways

- Remote ID applies to drones that are required to be registered or are registered, whether they are flown for fun, for business, or for public safety.
- There are three ways to comply: a Standard Remote ID drone, a drone with a broadcast module, or flying without broadcasting inside an FAA-recognized identification area (FRIA).
- The FAA registration page lists one exception: a drone of 0.55 pounds or less (under 250 grams) flown recreationally does not have to be registered, and therefore is not tied to Remote ID.
- The FAA's period of discretionary enforcement ended on March 16, 2024, so pilots of non-compliant aircraft can now face fines and action against their pilot certificates.
- Verify your own aircraft and your own situation with the FAA before flying. This page is a planning reference, not legal advice.

## What Remote ID actually broadcasts

Remote ID is often called a digital license plate. According to the FAA, a Standard Remote ID drone broadcasts a unique identifier (a serial number or a session ID), the drone's latitude, longitude, geometric altitude and velocity, the control station's latitude, longitude and geometric altitude, a time mark, and an emergency status indication. A drone with a broadcast module sends the module's serial number, the drone's position and velocity, the takeoff location, and a time mark. In both cases the broadcast runs from takeoff to shutdown.

The FAA says this information can be received by other parties. The FAA, law enforcement and other federal agencies use it to locate a control station, and authorized people from public safety organizations can ask the FAA who owns a drone. Session IDs can be linked to serial numbers by law enforcement and the FAA, but that link is not public. The FAA pages do not describe the radio technology or the range, so check your aircraft's manual instead of assuming a distance.

## Three ways to comply

The first way is a Standard Remote ID drone, which is built with the broadcast capability. The second is a drone fitted with a broadcast module, either a device or a software feature added to the aircraft; with this option the pilot must be able to see the drone at all times during the flight. The third is flying without Remote ID equipment, which is allowed only within visual line of sight and only inside a FAA-recognized identification area. FRIAs can be requested by community-based organizations and educational institutions. Our [FRIA guide](/en/guides/fria-explained) explains how they work.

One difference is worth noticing. The module reports the takeoff location, not the live position of the control station. A pilot who moves during the flight is therefore not tracked in the same way as with a Standard Remote ID aircraft, and the module's data should not be read as the operator's current position.

## How the 250-gram exemption works

The FAA's registration page says that most drones must be registered, and that the exception is a drone weighing 0.55 pounds or less (under 250 grams) that is flown recreationally. Remote ID follows registration: the FAA states that a drone that requires an FAA registration number must also broadcast Remote ID, unless it is flown inside a FRIA. A drone registered voluntarily is also covered by the Remote ID rule.

What matters is how you fly, not only the weight. A drone used under Part 107, for example for paid work, does not fall under the recreational exception, and a drone registered for recreation cannot be used under Part 107. Registration costs 5 US dollars and is valid for three years; the recreational registration covers every drone in your inventory, while a Part 107 registration is per aircraft. If your plans for a light drone include any work purpose, read our [Part 107 overview](/en/guides/faa-part-107-explained) first.

## Remote ID is a broadcast, not a tracking feed

Some pilots imagine that the FAA follows every drone in real time. The rule is built around a broadcast that nearby receivers can pick up and around the ability of the FAA and law enforcement to work with the data. It is not a public flight-history service. The FAA pages do not describe module power or range, so read your module's manual and check its power and settings before every flight.

## Deadlines and enforcement

The FAA's Remote ID page gives a compliance deadline of September 16, 2023. The FAA then announced that its discretionary enforcement policy would end on March 16, 2024. Since that date, operators who do not comply can face fines as well as suspension or revocation of their drone pilot certificates, in the FAA's own wording. Rules can change, so confirm the current text on the FAA site.

## Practical checklist

- Find out whether your aircraft is a Standard Remote ID drone, uses a broadcast module, or can only be flown inside a FRIA.
- If you use a module, check its power and its settings before each flight, and remember that you must keep the drone in sight.
- For a FRIA, look up the exact mapped boundary and confirm that your flight stays inside it.
- Do not assume that a light drone is exempt if the flight has any work purpose.
- Check the registration status and the expiry date of your registration.
- Verify the current rules directly with the FAA before flying.

## FAQ

### Does a drone I bought before the rule need Remote ID?

If it is required to be registered, or you registered it, the Remote ID rule applies to it. The FAA lists the same three compliance options for older aircraft: a built-in capability, a broadcast module, or a FRIA.

### Can anyone see where I am?

The FAA says other parties can receive Remote ID information. Public safety personnel can ask the FAA for an owner's identity, while the link between session IDs and serial numbers is not public.

### Does my sub-250-gram drone need Remote ID?

The FAA's registration page says a drone of 0.55 pounds or less flown recreationally does not need to be registered. If it does not need an FAA registration number, the Remote ID requirement that follows registration does not apply. Once the drone is used under Part 107 or is registered, the rule applies.

### Is Remote ID the same as registration?

No. Registration gives you an FAA number for the aircraft, while Remote ID is the broadcast of identity and position data during flight. The two are connected, because a drone that needs a registration number must also broadcast Remote ID.

Pilots who've only flown under a single national framework — the FAA in the US, or MLIT in Japan — often assume EASA works the same way: one agency, one rulebook, one point of contact. It doesn't quite. EASA sets the common regulation that applies across the EU and EEA, but registration, operational approvals, and day-to-day enforcement are handled by each country's own aviation authority — Spain's AESA, France's DGAC, Germany's LBA, and so on. A drone operator registered in one member state can fly in all the others on that same registration number, which is genuinely unusual compared to the US (a Part 107 certificate has zero standing in EU airspace) — but that portability covers *who* you are, not *where* you're allowed to fly on any given day.

## Three categories sorted by risk, not by drone size alone

The EU framework splits every flight into Open, Specific, or Certified, and the split is based on risk rather than weight class alone. Open covers low-risk flying within fixed limits — VLOS, altitude capped at 120m, and one of the A1/A2/A3 subcategories governing distance from uninvolved people — with no prior approval needed. Specific covers higher-risk operations, such as BVLOS flights or flying heavier drones over people, and requires either a self-declared Standard Scenario or a full risk assessment submitted to the national authority. Certified is reserved for operations carrying risk comparable to manned aviation, like drone delivery over populated areas, and follows a certification process closer to that of a conventional aircraft.

## The C0–C6 class mark changes what a drone is allowed to do, not just its weight

Every drone placed on the EU market since the class-marking system took effect carries a CE class label — C0 for under 250g, C1 for under 900g, C2 for under 4kg, C3 for under 25kg, and so on — and that label, not just the number on a scale, is what determines which Open subcategory a drone qualifies for. The mistake this produces: a drone that happens to weigh 230g but was privately built or imported before class marking applied doesn't automatically get the same A1 privileges as a shop-bought C0-labeled drone of the same weight. Older, unmarked aircraft are generally pushed toward the more conservative A3 distance rules under the transitional provisions, even when the number on the spec sheet looks identical to a newer, properly marked model.

## Registration is EU-wide, but it isn't a flight permit

Any drone over 250g, and any drone of any weight carrying a sensor able to capture personal data — in practice, almost any camera — needs an operator registration with a member state's aviation authority. That single number is valid across every EU and EEA country, so a pilot registered in, say, the Netherlands doesn't need to re-register before flying in Portugal. What it doesn't do is clear a specific flight for a specific location: national airspace restrictions, airport proximity rules, and local no-fly zones still apply exactly as if the pilot were a local, and registration status says nothing about whether today's flight at today's coordinates is legal.

## Specific category flights: Standard Scenarios and full SORA are two different doors to the same approval

For operations that don't fit Open, there are two routes. A Standard Scenario (STS-01 for VLOS, STS-02 for BVLOS with observers) lets an operator self-declare compliance against a predefined checklist, skipping the full assessment — but only if every listed condition matches exactly. The more common failure here isn't ignorance of the scenario; it's assuming a close-enough match counts. A planned BVLOS flight that relies on an observer network without truly continuous, direct visual contact with the aircraft doesn't satisfy STS-02's actual wording, even if it looks functionally similar on the day, and a self-declaration filed against a mismatched scenario is not a valid authorization. The alternative is a SORA (Specific Operations Risk Assessment), which quantifies ground and air risk and produces a mitigation plan submitted to the national authority for a case-by-case operational authorization — slower, but it fits flights that don't cleanly match any Standard Scenario.

## U-space exists, but it's not everywhere yet

U-space is the EU's framework for digital, often automated traffic management in designated low-altitude airspace volumes — requiring things like e-identification and geo-awareness, and in some zones, registering a flight plan with a U-space service provider before departure. It's a real and growing requirement, but it's currently limited to specific designated volumes around certain airports and urban corridors rather than a nationwide layer, so its absence from a given area today doesn't mean it stays absent as rollout continues.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Treating EASA's rulebook as the full picture without checking the destination country's own aviation authority for airspace maps and local procedures.
- Assuming a sub-250g drone with no class mark gets the same A1 treatment as a properly C0-labeled one.
- Self-declaring a Standard Scenario without checking every listed condition against the actual planned flight.
- Assuming one EU-wide registration number means blanket permission to fly anywhere in the EU without checking local restrictions.

## Practical checklist

- Confirm your operator registration is current with your home member state's authority, and keep the number on hand — it travels with you across the EU.
- Check your drone's class mark (C0–C6) before assuming which A1/A2/A3 subcategory applies; an unmarked legacy drone may default to A3's stricter distances.
- Work out whether your planned flight fits Open, or needs a Standard Scenario self-declaration or a full SORA submission under Specific.
- Before self-declaring STS-01 or STS-02, compare every listed condition to your actual flight plan line by line.
- Always verify current requirements, airspace maps, and U-space coverage through EASA and the destination country's own aviation authority — this guide is reference information only and does not substitute for an actual flight authorization.

Someone shopping for a first drone often compares a camera drone gliding over a coastline with an FPV drone diving through a stairwell and assumes the difference is the footage style. The bigger difference is how each aircraft is controlled and what it takes to fly one safely. This guide uses DJI's published specifications for the Mini 4 Pro and the Avata 2 as examples of the two categories, plus the line-of-sight rules of the FAA and Australia's CASA. Custom-built racing and freestyle quads vary widely, so we do not quote typical numbers for them.

## Key takeaways

- A camera drone like the Mini 4 Pro uses GNSS and sensors to hold its position. In the Manual mode of DJI's FPV aircraft, automatic stabilization is turned off and the pilot has to keep the aircraft level.
- DJI lists up to 34 minutes of flight time for the Mini 4 Pro, and a third-party specification summary lists about 23 minutes for the Avata 2. Both are measured in ideal conditions, and custom FPV builds can differ a lot.
- The Mini 4 Pro weighs under 249 grams with the standard battery. The Avata 2 is listed at a takeoff weight of about 377 grams in a third-party summary, so different rules can apply.
- The FAA says recreational pilots must keep the drone within visual line of sight or use a co-located visual observer. Australia's CASA says the drone must stay in your unaided view and notes that goggles reduce awareness.
- DJI recommends practicing in its Virtual Flight simulator before a first FPV flight.

## Control and stabilization

According to DJI's Avata flight-mode overview, in Normal mode the aircraft uses GNSS, downward vision and infrared sensors to hold its position, and in Sport mode it relies on GNSS and downward vision with quicker responses. In Manual mode, which is aimed at experienced pilots, automatic stabilization is turned off, so the pilot must keep the aircraft level and away from obstacles. A typical camera drone does not offer this mode, which is a large part of why it is easier to learn. Letting go of the sticks on a camera drone normally leaves it hovering, while in a manual mode it does not.

## Footage style follows the airframe

Camera drones are built for smooth, stable shots such as landscapes, exteriors and steady tracking shots. FPV aircraft are built for fast, agile movement close to subjects and obstacles, and the pilot flies by watching the live video in goggles. Each type fights its own design when asked to do the other's job. If your goal is steady establishing shots, a camera drone is the natural choice, and if you want fast, close, dynamic shots, FPV is the more suitable tool, provided you accept the learning curve.

## Flight time and weight

DJI's specifications give a feel for the two categories. For the Mini 4 Pro, DJI states up to 34 minutes with the standard battery, measured in a windless wind tunnel at a constant 21.6 km/h. For the Avata 2, a specification summary lists about 23 minutes measured at 21.6 km/h in calm air at sea level, with a note that the data is for reference only. The same summary lists a takeoff weight of about 377 grams for the Avata 2, and DJI states that the Mini 4 Pro is under 249 grams with its standard battery. Weight can decide which registration and operating rules apply to you, so look up the numbers for the exact aircraft you plan to buy. For a custom-built FPV drone, the flight time depends on the build, so check the specifications of the parts.

## Line-of-sight rules and goggles

The FAA's page for recreational flyers says that you must fly within visual line of sight so that you can see and avoid other aircraft, and that you may instead use a visual observer who is next to you and in direct communication. The page does not discuss FPV by name. Flying with goggles removes your direct view of the aircraft, so a spotter is the usual way to meet such a requirement, but you should confirm how it applies to you with the FAA. Australia's CASA states that you must always keep your drone within visual line of sight, says that FPV goggles reduce your awareness and raise the risk of collisions, and allows recreational FPV indoors if the drone cannot leave the building. Outdoors, check the options on CASA's page. Our guide to [VLOS and BVLOS](/en/guides/vlos-bvlos-explained) explains the terms.

## Common mistake: buying the wrong first aircraft

Beginners sometimes buy a camera drone expecting to do FPV-style acrobatics with it, or buy an FPV aircraft expecting easy establishing shots. Decide what you want to film first, then match the aircraft. If you want FPV, plan simulator time, and if you only want steady scenery, a camera drone avoids the learning curve. Our [beginner practice guide](/en/guides/beginner-drone-flying-practice-step-by-step) describes a sensible progression.

## Practical checklist

- Decide whether you need smooth establishing shots or fast, close, dynamic shots before you buy.
- Compare the manufacturer's flight time and weight for the exact aircraft, and remember that the figures are lab maxima.
- Learn how your country's rules treat goggles, line of sight and visual observers, and plan a spotter for FPV.
- Practice in a simulator before flying a manual mode near anything valuable.
- Start FPV in the most stabilized mode your aircraft offers, and change modes only when you can recover from mistakes.

## FAQ

### Is FPV flying harder than camera-drone flying?

In a manual mode, yes, because the pilot has to keep the aircraft level. DJI says to practice in its simulator before a first FPV flight.

### Do FPV drones fly as long as camera drones?

It depends on the model. DJI lists up to 34 minutes for the Mini 4 Pro, and a third-party specification summary lists about 23 minutes for the Avata 2, both in ideal conditions.

### Can I fly FPV alone?

The FAA says you must keep the drone in visual line of sight or use a visual observer who is next to you. CASA says the drone must stay in your unaided view. Check your own rules.

### Do different weight rules apply?

They can. The Mini 4 Pro is under 249 grams with the standard battery, and the Avata 2 is about 377 grams.

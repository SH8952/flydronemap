In bright daylight, a video shutter speed that looks natural is often impossible to reach without cutting light. A neutral-density (ND) filter does that cutting. This guide explains the rule behind the choice, shows how to calculate a strength instead of guessing, and uses published DJI pages and photography references. We do not give a table that claims to match every light condition, because the right strength depends on your camera, the scene and the time of day.

## Key takeaways

- The 180-degree shutter rule says the shutter speed should be about twice the frame rate: 1/48 second at 24 fps, usually set to 1/50, and 1/60 second at 30 fps.
- An ND filter number is a light-reduction factor: ND2 is 1 stop, ND4 is 2 stops, and every doubling adds one stop, so ND16 is 4 stops, ND32 is 5 and ND64 is 6.
- Some drone cameras offer little or no aperture control. The Mini 4 Pro specification lists a single aperture value, f/1.7.
- A variable ND filter can show a dark X-shaped pattern near its strongest setting, mainly on wide-angle lenses.
- DJI says to use official ND filters for its aircraft because they are designed to work with the gimbal camera's center of gravity, and not to use a filter that is too strong for the light.

## Why bright light calls for ND

At a low ISO and the narrowest practical aperture, a bright scene still needs a very fast shutter speed. Fast shutter speeds freeze each frame and make motion look choppy. The 180-degree rule keeps enough blur for smooth motion, so the shutter should be about double the frame rate. The photography site Lightstalking explains the rule and notes that this can be hard to reach in bright light, because the aperture can only be closed so far. DJI's own ND guide describes ND filters as sunglasses for the camera and cites the same rule for video. On a drone, where the lens often cannot be stopped down, the ND filter is the main control you have left.

## Calculate the strength instead of guessing

Work in stops. First set your frame rate and the shutter speed from the rule, for example 1/50 at 24 fps. Then look at what shutter speed the camera needs for a correct exposure at your base ISO in the current light, using auto exposure for a moment. The gap between those two shutter speeds, counted in doublings, is the number of stops you need to remove. For example, if the camera wants 1/1000 second and you want 1/50, the ratio is 20, which is a little more than four doublings, so you would need a little more than 4 stops. An ND16 (4 stops) or an ND32 (5 stops) gets you close, and a small ISO or shutter adjustment makes up the rest. This is an illustration of the method, not a rule for your scene. Check the result in the app before you record, using the histogram or zebra display if your camera has one. A filter that is too strong underexposes the picture and forces you to raise ISO, as DJI's guide warns.

## What the numbers mean

An ND factor describes how much light is blocked: ND4 passes one quarter of the light, which is 2 stops. Photographers also use optical density, where 0.3 is 1 stop and 0.6 is 2 stops. Manufacturers sometimes print different labels, so read the packaging and convert to stops yourself. DJI sells an ND 16/64/256 set for the Mini 4 Pro, which spans roughly 4, 6 and 8 stops. A set with spread-out values leaves gaps, so check the exposure after every swap.

## Where variable ND filters fall short

A variable ND filter combines two polarizing layers that rotate against each other. Near the maximum density the light passes unevenly, which produces dark diagonal bands, often called the X pattern. A guide by KF Concept says it is most likely near the Max setting and mainly on wide-angle lenses, and that it is most visible in uniform areas such as clear sky, water and snow. Its suggested fixes are to turn the ring back toward the minimum, to use a longer focal length, to avoid stacking a polarizer, and to use a fixed ND for more reduction. Because most drone lenses are wide, a fixed filter that you swap as the light changes is usually the safer choice, but test your own filter before an important job.

## Use filters your aircraft was designed for

DJI recommends official ND filters for its aircraft because they are designed to work with the center of gravity of the gimbal camera. Third-party filters may be fine, but they add weight at the front of a small gimbal. After mounting any filter, power on and confirm that the gimbal moves freely and passes its self-check before you take off, and press the filter lightly to make sure it is fully seated. Follow the instructions of the filter maker for fitting.

## Color, cleaning and storage

Some filters add a color cast, so record a short test clip and set the white balance manually if you see one. A fingerprint or dust on a filter can show up as flare or softness in bright highlights, so store each filter in its own slot of a case and clean it with a microfiber cloth. Our [gimbal and lens cleaning guide](/en/guides/drone-gimbal-maintenance-and-cleaning-guide) describes a safe order for cleaning a lens.

## Practical checklist

- Set the frame rate first and the shutter speed to about twice that, such as 1/50 at 24 fps.
- Work out the stops you need, choose the nearest ND value and check the exposure in the app.
- Do not use a filter that is stronger than the light needs.
- Prefer fixed ND filters for wide shots, and test a variable ND across its range before you trust it.
- Use the filters recommended for your aircraft, and check that the gimbal moves freely with the filter on.
- Press-check the filter before every takeoff.
- Store filters separately and clean them with a microfiber cloth.

## FAQ

### Why do I need an ND filter at all?

Bright light forces a fast shutter speed, which makes video look choppy. An ND filter lets you keep a slower shutter that matches the frame rate.

### Which ND number should I buy?

It depends on your camera and the light. Calculate the stops you need as shown above, and start with a small set that covers your usual conditions.

### Is a variable ND filter a good idea for a drone?

It is convenient, but near its strongest setting it can show an X pattern on wide lenses. A fixed set is the safer default.

### Do I need official filters?

DJI says official filters are designed for the gimbal camera's center of gravity. If you use others, check that the gimbal moves freely.

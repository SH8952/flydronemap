Pilots who've already flown in the US or Japan tend to bring one habit to Spain that doesn't transfer cleanly: checking a single agency's rulebook and assuming that covers it. Spain splits the question in two. AESA (the Spanish aviation safety agency) governs who can fly, what certificate they need, and how the drone must be registered. ENAIRE — the country's air navigation manager — publishes the ZGUAS map (Zonas Geográficas UAS) that decides where a given flight is actually allowed. A pilot can be fully registered and certified under AESA's rules and still be flying illegally simply because they never opened the ENAIRE map for that specific spot.

## Registration depends on weight and camera, not just weight

Under Spain's current framework (Real Decreto 517/2024, aligned with the EU's common UAS rules), registration as a drone operator with AESA is required once a drone's maximum take-off mass reaches 250g, and separately, any drone carrying a sensor capable of capturing personal data — in practice, almost any camera — must be registered regardless of weight. The exemption that trips people up is narrow: a sub-250g aircraft with no camera at all, essentially a toy-class model, is the only category that skips registration outright. A 199g consumer drone with a 4K camera still needs an AESA operator number, which pilots who fixate on "under 250g means no paperwork" routinely miss.

## A1, A2, and A3 are about distance from people, not skill level

Spain's open category follows the EU-wide structure, and the three subcategories are defined by how close you can legally get to uninvolved people rather than by drone size alone. A1 covers drones under roughly 250g (or CE class C0/C1) and permits flying close to people but never deliberately over uninvolved crowds. A2 requires the separate A2 remote pilot certificate and allows flight down to 30m horizontal distance from uninvolved people — or as little as 5m in a drone's low-speed mode, if the aircraft supports it and the pilot has verified that setting. A3 has no minimum-distance exemption for proximity to people at all; instead it requires staying at least 150m away from residential, commercial, industrial, and recreational areas entirely. The mistake this produces in practice: a pilot who only holds the A1/A3 certificate assumes any open field is fine, launches 150m from a house but well within a nearby park full of weekend visitors, and ends up in an A3 violation because the park counts as a recreational area under the rule, not just the house.

## ZGUAS is a legal requirement to check, not a convenience layer

The ENAIRE Drones platform's ZGUAS map shows prohibited zones (LEP), restricted zones active on specific schedules (LER), danger zones tied to known but non-constant risk such as military exercises (LED), and controlled airspace around airports and TMAs. Consulting this map before every flight isn't a best practice suggestion — it's a legal obligation under Spanish UAS operating rules, independent of which open-category subcategory the flight falls under. The general guidance published around these zones keeps drone operations roughly 8km from the nearest runway at major airports without prior coordination, though the actual boundary for any specific airport or heliport can differ from that baseline and has to be confirmed on the map itself rather than assumed from a rule of thumb. A field that looks completely rural on a standard map can still sit inside a TMA approach corridor that only becomes visible once ZGUAS is actually consulted.

## The drone's own geofencing is not the same as the legal restriction

A separate, common point of confusion: many consumer drones ship with manufacturer geofencing (DJI's GEO system is the most common example in Spain) that blocks or warns about takeoff in certain areas. That system is configured by the manufacturer, not by AESA or ENAIRE, and the two don't always match — a spot can be legally restricted under ZGUAS while the drone's own software raises no warning at all, or the reverse, where the manufacturer's zone is more conservative than the actual legal boundary. Treating "the app let me take off" as confirmation that a flight is legal is one of the more persistent mistakes pilots new to Spain make, because it substitutes a private company's caution level for the actual regulatory answer that only the ZGUAS map provides.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Assuming a sub-250g drone never needs registration, without checking whether it has a camera.
- Treating the A1/A3 certificate as sufficient for any open field, without accounting for A3's 150m distance from residential, commercial, industrial, and recreational areas.
- Skipping the ZGUAS check because a location looks rural, instead of confirming there's no TMA, LER, or LED zone overhead.
- Relying on a drone's built-in geofencing warnings as a substitute for checking ZGUAS, when the two systems aren't guaranteed to agree.

## Practical checklist

- Confirm whether your drone needs AESA operator registration based on both its weight and whether it carries a camera.
- Identify which open-category subcategory (A1, A2, or A3) your flight actually fits, and hold the matching certificate.
- Check the ENAIRE Drones ZGUAS map for your exact coordinates before every flight, not just once per general area.
- Don't treat your drone's manufacturer geofencing behavior as confirmation that a zone is legally clear.
- Always verify current requirements and the live ZGUAS map through AESA and ENAIRE's official channels — this guide is reference information only and does not substitute for an actual flight authorization.

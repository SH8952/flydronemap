Most weather apps compress the whole day into a single word — "clear," "cloudy," "rain" — but the numbers that matter to a drone pilot are hiding behind that summary. "Clear" at 5 m/s and "clear" at 15 m/s are two completely different flights, and "partly cloudy" with a low cloud base can change what you are allowed to do under the rules of your country. Here is a practical rundown of what to check, and in what order.

## Key takeaways

- Look at the gust figure, not only the average wind. The US National Weather Service (NWS) defines a gust as a rapid fluctuation of wind speed with variations of 10 knots (about 5 m/s) or more between peaks and lulls.
- A "30% chance of rain" is a probability of measurable rain at a point, not a statement about how heavy it will be. Check intensity and timing as well, and treat thunderstorms as a separate no-fly signal.
- Visibility and cloud base are legal limits as well as safety limits. In the US, Part 107 asks for at least 3 statute miles of visibility and 500 feet of vertical distance below clouds.
- Temperature, dew point and the Kp index each need their own look. Check the limits in your own aircraft's manual instead of assuming a generic range.
- Re-check right before takeoff: forecasts are updated, and conditions can change within the window you plan to fly.

## Where the numbers come from

Pick one or two sources and use them the same way every time. A national weather service forecast (in the US, the NWS hourly forecast and its forecast discussion), the weather panel of your flight app, and the FlyDroneMap [dashboard](/en) all give you current conditions. The dashboard also shows a 24-hour hourly timeline and a best flying window, which is useful because the question is rarely "is it fine right now?" but "will it stay fine for the 20 minutes I need?". Whatever you use, look at the hourly values for your actual flight window, not the daily summary.

## Wind: check the gust number, not just the average

The average wind is the number most apps show first, but it is the gusts that disturb an aircraft. NWS defines a gust as a rapid fluctuation of wind speed with variations of 10 knots or more between peaks and lulls. In practice, a forecast showing 6 m/s average with gusts of 12 m/s describes a day when the aircraft will repeatedly face wind twice as strong as the average suggests.

Compare the gust, not the average, with the wind limit published for your aircraft. Limits differ by model and are stated in the manufacturer's documents; the guide on [wind resistance by drone model](/en/guides/drone-wind-resistance-by-model) collects official figures. Remember too that wind close to the ground and wind at flight height are not the same thing, so a calm launch site does not guarantee calm air at 100 m.

## Precipitation: probability is not intensity

The NWS defines probability of precipitation (PoP) as the chance of at least 0.01 inch of precipitation at any given point in the forecast area during the stated period. It combines how confident the forecaster is that precipitation will form with how much of the area it will cover. So "30%" tells you about likelihood at a point, not about how hard it will rain or for how long.

For drone flying, look for the expected intensity and the hour in which precipitation is most likely, then compare with your aircraft's rating. Many consumer aircraft are not rated for water, and manufacturer safety guidelines, for example DJI's, list rain among the conditions in which not to fly. If any thunderstorm is in the forecast for your window, treat that as a reason to move the flight: see [lightning and thunderstorm safety](/en/guides/lightning-and-thunderstorm-drone-safety).

## Visibility and cloud base are legal limits

In the United States, 14 CFR 107.51 says the minimum flight visibility, observed from the location of the control station, must be at least 3 statute miles (about 4.8 km), and the aircraft must stay at least 500 feet below clouds and at least 2,000 feet horizontally from clouds. Other countries set their own figures, so check the regulator for the country where you fly.

A practical consequence of the cloud rule: with the aircraft at 400 feet above ground, the cloud base would need to be at least 900 feet above ground to keep the 500-foot vertical gap. A low ceiling can therefore reduce your usable altitude even when the legal height cap is higher. Fog and haze can cut visibility quickly around dusk and dawn.

## Temperature: read it against your battery's limits

Temperature limits are model-specific. As one example, DJI's Air 3S manual gives an operating temperature of -10 to 40 degrees C, says battery capacity is significantly reduced when flying at temperatures between -10 and 5 degrees C, and recommends warming the battery to at least 10 degrees C before takeoff in low temperatures. It also states that the battery only charges between 5 and 40 degrees C. Other aircraft have other limits, so use the figures in your own manual.

## Dew point: the gap matters more than the humidity

NWS fog guidance notes that fog forms when temperature and dew point approach the same value, with a spread of less than 5 degrees F (about 3 degrees C). A small temperature-dew point spread on the forecast is therefore a cue to expect fog or condensation, especially in calm, clear conditions. ## Space weather is a separate check

Ground weather says nothing about space weather. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center describes the planetary K-index (Kp) as an integer from 0 to 9, where 1 is calm and 5 or more indicates a geomagnetic storm; Kp 5 corresponds to the G1 level on NOAA's scale. NOAA's scale table lists no navigation effects at the G1 level and mentions satellite-navigation problems only at higher levels (for example, intermittent problems at G3), so a Kp of 5 is not proof of a GPS problem. Treating it as a reason to be more cautious on long-range flights or flights that rely on automatic return-to-home is a conservative habit, not a rule. Read [what the Kp index is](/en/guides/what-is-the-kp-index) before deciding on your own threshold.

## A simple order of checks

| Step | What to look at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thunderstorm risk | Overrides everything else |
| 2 | Gusts for the flight window | Decides whether the aircraft can hold position |
| 3 | Precipitation timing and intensity | Most consumer aircraft are not rated for water |
| 4 | Visibility and cloud base | Legal limits and your ability to see the aircraft |
| 5 | Temperature and dew point | Battery performance and fogging |
| 6 | Kp index | Navigation reliability on longer flights |

## Common mistakes

- Using one forecast number for the whole day. Look at the hours you will actually fly.
- Reading the average wind as the safe number when gusts are much higher.
- Taking a low rain percentage as permission to fly into a shower.
- Using a generic temperature range instead of the one in your aircraft's manual.
- Checking the forecast the night before and not again at the launch site.

## Preflight checklist

- Gusts for the whole window are below your aircraft's stated wind limit with margin.
- No thunderstorms forecast; precipitation timing and intensity checked.
- Visibility and cloud base meet your local legal minimums, checked for takeoff and landing time.
- Temperature is inside your manual's limits; battery warmed if it is cold.
- Temperature-dew point spread checked for fog or lens condensation.
- Kp index checked for longer flights.

## FAQ

### Which single number matters most?

For most flights it is the gust speed in your flight window, compared with the wind limit of your aircraft. A calm average can hide short bursts that are much stronger.

### What does a 30% chance of rain mean?

In NWS terms, it is a 30% chance of at least 0.01 inch of rain at a given point during the forecast period. It does not tell you how heavy the rain will be.

### How low can the clouds be for a legal flight?

It depends on the country. In the US, Part 107 requires the aircraft to stay at least 500 feet below clouds and 2,000 feet horizontally away from them, plus at least 3 statute miles of visibility. Check your own regulator.

### Do I need to check Kp for a short local flight?

NOAA's Kp is a global indicator of geomagnetic activity. It is most relevant for longer flights or flights that depend heavily on GPS and automatic return-to-home. A quick look takes seconds.

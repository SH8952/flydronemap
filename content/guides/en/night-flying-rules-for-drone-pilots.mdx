This guide covers night flights under the FAA's Part 107 rules in the United States. A pilot shooting a dusk property video can cross from daylight into twilight and then into night without changing anything about the drone, which is why the legal line deserves more attention than the clock.

## Key takeaways

- Under 14 CFR 107.29, a small drone may fly at night without a waiver if the remote pilot has completed the required knowledge test or training after April 6, 2021, and the aircraft has anti-collision lighting.
- The light must be visible for at least 3 statute miles and flash at a rate sufficient to avoid a collision.
- The same lighting is required during civil twilight, not only in full darkness.
- Civil twilight outside Alaska is the 30 minutes before official sunrise and the 30 minutes after official sunset.
- The pilot may dim the light when operating conditions make that safer, but may not turn it off.
- Visual line of sight rules still apply, and your vision must be unaided other than by corrective lenses.

## What the rule says

Section 107.29 of Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations sets two conditions for night operations. First, the remote pilot in command must have completed an initial knowledge test or training under 107.65, as applicable, after April 6, 2021. Second, the aircraft must carry lighted anti-collision lighting that is visible for at least 3 statute miles and flashes at a rate sufficient to avoid a collision. The old practice of applying for a night waiver is no longer the route for ordinary night flights: night operations under certificates of waiver issued before April 21, 2021 stopped being allowed after May 17, 2021.

The training condition is the part pilots overlook. A certificate holder from before the change has to complete the updated training that covers night operations before flying after dark. Check the FAA's current material for the exact course that applies to you.

## Civil twilight needs lights too

It is a common belief that the lighting rule starts only when it is fully dark. The text says otherwise. During civil twilight, 107.29 requires the same anti-collision lighting: visible for at least 3 statute miles with a flash rate sufficient to avoid a collision. Outside Alaska, civil twilight is defined as the 30 minutes before official sunrise and the 30 minutes after official sunset. In Alaska it is the civil twilight in the Air Almanac. So the dusk flight in the opening example needs the light as soon as the sun has set, not once the sky is black.

## About the light itself

The rule is written around distance, not around a product. A small decorative LED that can be seen from a short way off may not meet the 3-mile standard, so read the specifications of the light you buy and compare them with the text of the rule. The pilot may reduce the intensity of the lighting if conditions make that safer, but the lighting may not be extinguished.

## The limits of eyesight at night

Under 14 CFR 107.31 the pilot in command, the person at the controls and any visual observer must see the aircraft with vision unaided by any device other than corrective lenses. Glasses and contact lenses are allowed, but binoculars or cameras are not. The purpose is to know where the aircraft is, judge its attitude, altitude and direction, watch the airspace for other traffic and confirm that it is not endangering anyone. In low light it is harder to judge distance and altitude, and thin obstacles such as wires are hard to see. Plan the route and the launch site in daylight first, and keep a clear margin above obstacles.

## Other rules do not pause at sunset

Every airspace and land-use rule that applies during the day still applies at night. A temporary flight restriction does not depend on the time of day, and the National Park Service rules on drones do not change with the light either. Our guides on [temporary flight restrictions](/en/guides/temporary-flight-restrictions-tfr-explained) and on [national parks](/en/guides/flying-drones-in-national-parks-rules) explain how those work. In controlled airspace, make sure your authorization and its conditions match the flight you actually plan, and read our [LAANC guide](/en/guides/what-is-laanc-and-why-it-matters). Remote ID rules also still apply, see [Remote ID requirements](/en/guides/remote-id-requirements-explained). Local ordinances may add limits of their own.

## Recreational flyers

This page describes Part 107. The FAA's recreational flyer page says recreational pilots must pass the Recreational UAS Safety Test, carry proof, keep the drone within visual line of sight and follow the safety guidelines of an FAA-recognized community-based organization. It does not address night flying, so if you fly for fun, read your organization's guidelines and the FAA's recreational page before a night flight.

## Practical checklist

- Confirm that your training or knowledge test meets the 107.29 condition before a night flight.
- Fit an anti-collision light that is visible for at least 3 statute miles and flashes at a suitable rate.
- Look up civil twilight and sunset times for your location and date, and turn the light on from the start of twilight.
- Keep the drone within your own unaided line of sight for the whole flight.
- Scout the site in daylight, note wires and other obstacles, and set a safe altitude.
- Re-check airspace authorizations, temporary flight restrictions and land-use rules for the time of your flight.
- Confirm the current rules with the FAA before flying. This page is a planning reference.

## FAQ

### Do I still need a waiver to fly at night?

For Part 107 operations, 107.29 allows night flights if you have completed the required knowledge test or training after April 6, 2021 and the aircraft has the anti-collision lighting. The old night waivers issued before April 21, 2021 stopped being valid after May 17, 2021.

### Is the light needed at dusk?

Yes. Paragraph (b) requires the same anti-collision lighting during civil twilight. Outside Alaska that is the 30 minutes before official sunrise and the 30 minutes after official sunset.

### Can I dim or switch off the light?

You may reduce its intensity if conditions make that safer. You may not turn it off.

### Does this apply to hobby flights?

Not in the same way. Recreational flyers follow the recreational rules and the guidelines of a community-based organization, and the FAA page does not mention night flying, so check both sources.

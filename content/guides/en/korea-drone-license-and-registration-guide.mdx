The two things that trip up pilots flying in South Korea most often are figuring out whether their drone even needs to be registered, and realizing that registration, a pilot certificate, and flight authorization are three separate approvals — not one bundled step. Pilots who check only one of the three and skip the others are the ones who get stopped mid-shoot. Here's the order these actually work in, from the weight threshold to the specific portal you file through.

## 250 grams isn't a blanket exemption

South Korea splits registration duty at 250 grams of maximum takeoff weight. Anything over that has to be registered through the Drone One-Stop portal (drone.onestop.go.kr); anything under it carries no registration duty. The detail that trips people up is what "maximum takeoff weight" actually means — it's the drone's full weight at liftoff, batteries, gimbal, and any prop guards included, not just the bare airframe weight printed on the spec sheet. A drone listed at 249g can easily cross 250g once a spare battery or an aftermarket guard is mounted, and pilots who only check the spec sheet number are the ones who get caught off guard. And even under 250g with no registration required, the basic flight rules — no flying over crowds, altitude limits — still apply; being under the threshold exempts you from paperwork, not from the rules themselves.

## Which certificate class you need is set by weight, not intent

Korea's pilot certificate splits into four classes by maximum takeoff weight: Class 4 (over 250g up to 2kg) only requires online training; Class 3 (over 2kg up to 7kg) needs at least 6 hours of logged flight time plus written and practical exams; Class 2 (over 7kg up to 25kg) needs 10 hours; and Class 1 (over 25kg, up to 150kg empty weight) needs 20 hours. The minimum age differs too — 14 for Classes 1 through 3, 10 for Class 4. The mistake pilots commonly make here is assuming "it's just a hobby drone, so no certificate needed" — the actual threshold is the aircraft's weight, not what you're using it for, so a hobbyist flying a DJI Air 3 or Mavic 3-class drone purely for fun still needs a Class 3 certificate if the weight crosses that line.

## Three situations that require separate flight authorization

Having both registration and a certificate in hand doesn't cover everything — flight authorization is a separate approval required for any flight above 150 meters altitude, any flight in controlled airspace near airports, and any flight in pre-designated restricted or prohibited zones, which in Seoul includes areas overlapping presidential security zones or nearby controlled airspace. A wide-open-looking park in central Seoul can still sit inside one of these zones on the map, which is exactly why "it looks empty, it should be fine" is one of the more common ways pilots end up in an authorization violation without realizing it. Authorization requests also go through the Drone One-Stop portal, and since processing time and the reviewing authority can vary by zone and altitude, filing with a comfortable buffer before your planned flight date is worth the extra day.

## Aerial photography needs its own permit, separate from flight authorization

This is the step pilots miss most — having flight authorization does not automatically clear you to shoot photos or video. Capturing aerial imagery requires a separate aerial photography application, processed nationally, and it's meant to be filed at least 4 business days before the shoot date, which doesn't leave room for a same-day spontaneous shoot. Approval is typically valid for a year once granted, but that validity is often tied to a specific location or radius, so moving to a different site for a follow-up shoot can require reapplying even while the original approval is still technically active.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Checking the spec-sheet airframe weight instead of the actual takeoff weight with batteries and accessories attached, and misjudging registration status as a result.
- Getting flight authorization but skipping the separate aerial photography application before shooting.
- Assuming a quiet-looking area in a major city like Seoul is unrestricted without checking whether it overlaps controlled or security airspace.
- Confusing the certificate requirement with "hobby vs. commercial use" instead of checking the actual weight-based threshold.

## Practical checklist

- Calculate your drone's real takeoff weight — batteries and accessories included — before assuming it's under 250g.
- Match that weight to the correct certificate class and its required flight hours.
- File for flight authorization through the Drone One-Stop portal ahead of time if you'll be above 150m or inside a restricted zone.
- If you plan to shoot photo or video, file the separate aerial photography application at least 4 business days in advance.
- Always confirm current requirements and final approval through the official Drone One-Stop portal (drone.onestop.go.kr) — this guide is reference information only and does not substitute for an actual flight approval.

Picture two operators filing waiver requests the same week: a pipeline inspection company that needs to fly a 6-mile corridor no visual observer chain can realistically keep eyes on, and a mapping crew that wants to launch from the bed of a truck moving down a rural access road. Both operations are legal only with an FAA Part 107 waiver, and both are the kind of request where the application stands or falls on the safety explanation: the FAA says it will disapprove an application that does not identify operational hazards and propose risk mitigations, however safe the flight might be in practice.

## Key takeaways

- A waiver lets a certificated remote pilot deviate from specific Part 107 rules (the list is in 14 CFR 107.205) once the FAA finds the operation can be conducted safely under the waiver's terms.
- The application is built around a safety explanation: describe the operation, name the hazards the waived rule normally controls, and show how your mitigations replace that function.
- The FAA aims to decide within 90 days but says timing varies with complexity, so file early and scope the request narrowly.
- Routine night flight and flight over people no longer need a waiver, and airspace authorization near airports is a separate process.
- BVLOS has been handled by waiver under 107.31, and the FAA has also proposed a separate BVLOS rule. Check the FAA for its current status.

## What a waiver actually authorizes

Under 14 CFR 107.200, the FAA may issue a certificate of waiver if it finds that a proposed operation can safely be conducted under the terms of that certificate. The request must include a complete description of the operation and a justification that establishes it can be conducted safely, and the FAA may add limitations it considers necessary. A holder may deviate from Part 107 only to the extent the waiver specifies and must comply with every condition in it.

The regulations that can be waived are listed in 14 CFR 107.205:

| Section | Subject |
|---|---|
| 107.25 | Operation from a moving vehicle or aircraft |
| 107.29(a)(2) and (b) | Anti-collision light at night and in civil twilight |
| 107.31 | Visual line of sight (VLOS) |
| 107.33 | Visual observer |
| 107.35 | Operation of multiple small unmanned aircraft |
| 107.37(a) | Yielding the right of way |
| 107.39 | Operation over people |
| 107.41 | Operation in certain airspace |
| 107.51 | Operating limitations (speed, altitude, visibility, cloud distance) |
| 107.145 | Operations over moving vehicles |

Two limits are written into the rule itself: the FAA will not issue a waiver of 107.25 or 107.31 to allow carrying property of another for compensation or hire. Sections that are not on the list cannot be waived through this process.

## Do you still need a waiver? Night, people and airspace

Before drafting anything, check whether the rule already covers what you want.

- Night flight. The FAA's section-specific evaluation information notes that rulemaking now allows night operations without a waiver. Routine night flight under 107.29 needs recent training and anti-collision lighting visible for 3 statute miles, as explained in our [Part 107 overview](/en/guides/faa-part-107-explained). A waiver of the lighting requirement is still possible.
- Flight over people and moving vehicles. The same FAA document says rulemaking allows operations over human beings without a waiver, through the four-category system. Waivers can still be requested for aircraft that do not qualify.
- Airspace near airports. Authorization to fly in Class B, C, D or surface Class E airspace is requested through LAANC or FAADroneZone, as covered in our [LAANC guide](/en/guides/what-is-laanc-and-why-it-matters). Section 107.41 does appear in the waivable list, but the FAA's evaluation document says its Flight Standards office does not adjudicate 107.41 applications; for a combined operation you submit the waiver request and a separate airspace request that references the waiver number. Filing the wrong request type is a common first-time mistake.

## How to apply

The FAA's waiver page describes a three-step process: decide which regulation you need to waive, write the waiver safety explanation (operations, risks, mitigations), and submit through your Aviation Safety Hub account. The page says new applications use the Aviation Safety Hub rather than FAADroneZone, and previously submitted waivers remain in FAADroneZone.

According to the FAA's user guide:

1. Create an account: choose the customer user type, register with Login.gov including identity verification, and link it to DOT/FAA MyAccess.
2. Fill in operator information, the proposed operation (aircraft details, dates, location coordinates and description) and the operational questions. The system adds the regulations that apply based on your answers.
3. Complete the safety explanation and attach supporting material. A Concept of Operations document is recommended, along with maps, diagrams and procedures.
4. Track status from the dashboard until the application is resolved as issued, denied or canceled.

Useful limits from the FAA's materials: the user guide recommends a start date at least 90 days away and an end date no more than 48 months after the start. The FAA's application instructions say an application cannot be amended after submission, and that if the FAA asks for more information you have 30 days to respond or the request is canceled. They also give limits such as a 15,000-character safety explanation and up to 10 supporting documents, so check the current form for the figures that apply. You do not need to register a drone to request a waiver, but you must register it before any operation.

## Building a safety case that survives review

The application is not asking you to prove nothing can go wrong. It asks you to name the specific hazard the waived rule normally controls and show your mitigation replaces that function. The FAA's Waiver Safety Explanation Guidelines ask for:

- Operational details: coordinates and maps, maximum altitude, airspace class, airspace within 5 miles, and the type of area (rural, populated and so on).
- Aircraft details: type and power source, flight time, range, speed, dimensions and weight with payload, containment methods such as geofencing or a tether, and termination systems.
- Personnel: minimum experience for the remote pilot in command, crew size, training for observers and crew, how competency is tested, and recurrent training.
- Risk and mitigation: how the operation stays safe "even in unusual circumstances", through limits, technology, training, equipment, personnel or restricted-access areas.

The FAA also publishes a searchable list of issued waivers, with a PDF linked for each one. Reading waivers granted for your exact regulation section shows the conditions the FAA has actually attached.

## What the FAA looks at for specific sections

The FAA's section-specific evaluation information describes what reviewers look for. Examples:

| Waiver | Focus of the evaluation |
|---|---|
| 107.25 moving vehicle | Hazards of a dynamic environment, obstructions to line of sight, distractions, and communication failure; lost-link procedures must keep compliance with 107.31 and avoid flight over people |
| 107.31 BVLOS | Awareness of altitude, attitude and movement; detecting and avoiding aircraft; conspicuity; crew knowledge; command-and-control links |
| 107.35 multiple aircraft | Operating location or performance criteria so a failure does not put people or property at undue hazard; an example buffer is at least a 1-to-1 ratio of maximum altitude to distance from the edge of the area |
| 107.51(a) speed over 100 mph | Showing the extra hazard does not degrade safety, for example through restricted access |
| 107.51(c) and (d) reduced visibility or cloud clearance | Waivers are valid for Class G only and cannot be combined with other waivers or authorizations; reviewers want a way to comply with 107.37 and visibility measured at the control station |

### BVLOS in more detail

For 107.31 the FAA document describes several approaches: operating behind obstructions while still within VLOS distance, a chain of visual observers, airspace surveillance, or technology-based detect and avoid. It states that, at the time of publication, the FAA had not approved any technology as sufficient to detect and avoid other aircraft, and that command-and-control links should not rely on systems operating under Part 5 or Part 15 of the FCC rules, which operate on an interference basis. For visual observers, it says a person is generally effective at detecting most aircraft heading toward them at about 1.5 statute miles, with 2 to 2.5 miles for aircraft on tangential paths, and evaluators subtract the distance flown during detection, communication, decision and maneuver time from that figure. Ground-based radar must have an FCC grant. A waiver for 107.33 is not issued by itself; it is normally paired with 107.31.

The FAA has also proposed a dedicated BVLOS rule. A page dated August 6, 2025 describes it as a proposed rule, and the fact sheet describes permits for lower-risk operations and certificates for higher-risk ones. Because rulemaking changes, check the FAA's BVLOS pages for the current position before planning around it.

## Mistakes that add months to the timeline

- Leaving out hazards and mitigations. The FAA says this alone leads to disapproval.
- Scoping the waiver wider than the mission needs. A single route, altitude band and time window is easier to analyze than standing authority everywhere.
- Treating the filing as a same-month formality. The FAA says it will do its best to decide within 90 days, and complexity affects that.
- Leaving contingencies unaddressed. Lost link, GPS-denied navigation and an unauthorized person entering the area each need a named response.
- Asking for something the rule already allows, or the wrong kind of request, such as an airspace authorization filed as a waiver.

## Practical checklist

- Confirm the limitation you need lifted is in the 107.205 list, and that routine night flight or flight over people under the current rules does not already cover it.
- Treat airspace access near an airport as a separate LAANC or authorization process.
- Scope the request to exactly what the mission needs.
- Search the FAA's issued-waiver list for approved requests under your regulation section before drafting.
- Give lost-link, GPS-denied and unauthorized-person scenarios each a specific response procedure.
- File at least 90 days ahead of the operation, and more for complex or multi-section requests.
- Use the [FlyDroneMap dashboard](/en) and the [US regulations page](/en/regulations/us) for planning only. A granted waiver binds you to its stated conditions, and this site does not verify or confirm FAA authorization status.

## FAQ

### How long does a Part 107 waiver take?

The FAA says it will do its best to approve or disapprove requests within 90 days of submission, and that processing varies with complexity. If it asks for more information you have 30 days to respond.

### Do I need a waiver to fly at night?

Not for routine night flight if you meet the 107.29 conditions (recent training and anti-collision lighting). A waiver is relevant if you cannot meet a condition such as the lighting requirement.

### Can a waiver let me carry packages beyond visual line of sight?

No for 107.25 and 107.31 when property of another is carried for compensation or hire: the rule says no waiver of those sections will be issued for that purpose.

### Where do I apply?

According to the FAA, new waiver applications go through the Aviation Safety Hub with a Login.gov account; older applications remain in FAADroneZone.

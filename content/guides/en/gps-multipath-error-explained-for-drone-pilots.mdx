If your drone has ever drifted sideways while hovering next to a glass office tower or over a calm lake, with a clean satellite lock and a quiet Kp index, the cause probably wasn't space weather at all. It was multipath error, a GPS problem that is easy to misdiagnose because the symptoms can look just like ionospheric interference.

## Key takeaways

- Multipath happens when reflected copies of a satellite signal reach the antenna along with the direct one. It is a local, physical effect, so it has nothing to do with the Kp index.
- The typical fingerprint is repeatability: the same drift at the same spot, on quiet days as well as stormy ones, often while the satellite count still looks healthy.
- ESA's Navipedia describes typical code multipath errors as under 2 to 3 meters, with values above 15 meters difficult to observe. Treat multipath as a source of position wobble, not as a guaranteed large error.
- The practical fix is geometry: launch away from reflective surfaces, keep a wide view of the sky, and move the aircraft or the launch point rather than blaming the GPS module.
- Multipath, sky blockage and ionospheric disturbance can look alike from the cockpit. The pattern in place and time tells them apart.

## What multipath error actually is

A GPS signal travels in a straight line from satellite to receiver, but reflective surfaces such as glass facades, water, wet pavement, metal roofing or a parked car can bounce a copy of the same signal along a longer, indirect path. ESA's Navipedia defines multipath as interference generated when a signal arrives at the antenna by different ways. The receiver can end up mixing the direct signal with one or more delayed reflections, and because the reflected path is longer, the measured distance to that satellite is wrong. With several satellites affected, the calculated position shifts.

## How large can the error get?

Many guides quote dramatic numbers, so it helps to keep the sources in view. Navipedia explains that the theoretical maximum for code multipath on the GPS civil signal is very large (hundreds of meters), but that values above 15 meters are difficult to observe in practice and the typical error is under 2 to 3 meters. It also notes that code multipath is usually worst when the satellite is low above the horizon, because signals from low-elevation satellites are the most exposed to nearby reflecting structures.

For context, GPS.gov reports that high-quality single-frequency receivers reach a horizontal accuracy of roughly 2.2 meters or better 95 percent of the time, and it names three main things that degrade what users actually see: atmospheric effects, sky blockage and receiver quality. A small consumer drone's antenna and receiver are not the same as a survey-grade setup, so your own numbers will differ. The useful lesson is that a few meters of wobble near a reflector is plausible, while a drone that suddenly jumps by tens of meters deserves a second look at other causes.

## Where multipath is most likely

| Environment | Why it hurts | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Glass or metal-clad building faces | Large flat reflectors send delayed copies toward the antenna | Launch several meters away from the wall and hold position away from it |
| Calm water, docks, piers | Water surfaces can reflect the signal back toward the antenna | Take off from solid ground back from the edge when possible |
| Narrow streets between tall buildings | Reflections from both sides plus blocked sky | Expect weaker position hold and fly with extra margin |
| Parking structures, metal roofs, vehicles | Reflectors right next to the antenna | Move to open ground before takeoff |

## Multipath compared with other GPS problems

| Cause | Where it shows up | Timing | Typical clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multipath | One specific spot near reflectors | Any day, any Kp value | Repeats every time at the same corner |
| Sky blockage | Under trees, bridges, between buildings | Any day | Fewer satellites, warnings before takeoff |
| Ionospheric disturbance | Wide area | During a storm or, near the equator, after sunset | Many pilots in the region affected at once |
| Jamming or spoofing | Around a transmitter | Can start and stop abruptly | See the guide on [GPS jamming and spoofing](/en/guides/gps-jamming-and-spoofing-explained-for-drone-pilots) |

The distinguishing clue between multipath and space weather is location and repeatability. Ionosphere-driven trouble affects a wide area and follows a time window; multipath is tied to a fixed spot. If your drone drifts at the same corner of a parking structure every time you fly there, regardless of the day's Kp reading, multipath is the likelier cause. ## Common mistakes

- Launching directly beside the reflective surface itself, such as from a rooftop pad ringed by glass railings or a dock at the edge of open water, because that is where reflections are strongest relative to the direct signal.
- Trusting position hold between two tall buildings. Reflections from both facades combine with partially blocked sky, and the spread of visible satellites that GPS accuracy depends on gets worse.
- Reading a healthy satellite count as proof of a healthy position. Multipath errors can be present while the count and the signal bars look normal.
- Blaming space weather without checking whether the problem repeats at the same place. Repeatability points to the site.
- Treating one bad flight at a reflective site as a hardware fault and replacing parts, when moving the launch point would have solved it.

## How to reduce multipath in practice

- Launch and land from open ground a few meters away from glass, water or large metal surfaces whenever the site allows it.
- Navipedia lists better antenna quality and moving the antenna away from reflecting objects as the main ways to reduce multipath. On a small drone you cannot change the antenna, so the second option becomes your tool: choose the launch point and the flight line accordingly.
- Wait a minute or two after power-up in a reflective area and watch whether the drift in the position readout settles before takeoff.
- If drift appears only in one specific spot, treat it as a strong sign of multipath, then climb or move away from the surface and see whether it clears.
- For mapping or inspection near water, glass or metal-clad structures, check some image overlaps manually instead of trusting the stitched result blindly, since position tags may be slightly off in those areas.

## FAQ

### Can multipath error happen when the Kp index is low?

Yes. Multipath is caused by reflections near the aircraft, not by the ionosphere, so it happens just as easily on geomagnetically quiet days.

### Will my drone warn me about multipath?

Not necessarily. The satellite count and signal indicator can look normal because the satellites are still being tracked. Behavior, such as unexpected drift at one spot, is often the better clue.

### Is a bigger satellite count a cure for multipath?

No. More satellites can help the geometry, but reflections from a nearby wall or water surface can still pull the solution toward the reflector.

### Does multipath only affect small drones?

No. It affects any GNSS receiver. Navipedia notes that better antennas and moving away from reflecting objects are the standard remedies, which is why survey crews also pay attention to antenna sites.

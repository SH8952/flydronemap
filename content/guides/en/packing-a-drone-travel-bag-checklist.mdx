The drone body is rarely what causes problems when you travel. It is the batteries, the loose spare parts and the assumptions about what you could buy after landing. This guide uses the FAA's passenger rules for lithium batteries as a baseline, notes one national example from Korea, and keeps everything else to practical habits. Your airline and the aviation authority of your departure country have the last word.

## Key takeaways

- The FAA says spare lithium batteries must be carried in carry-on baggage only, not in checked baggage.
- For rechargeable lithium ion batteries the FAA's limit is 100 watt hours per battery. With airline approval, a passenger may carry up to two spare larger batteries of 101 to 160 watt hours. Larger batteries are not allowed.
- Battery terminals must be protected from short circuit, for example with retail packaging, tape, a battery case or a plastic bag.
- Airlines can set stricter limits than the FAA, so ask your carrier before you travel.
- Check the watt-hour rating printed on each battery before booking, and read your drone's manual for storage and transport advice.

## What the FAA says about batteries

The FAA's PackSafe pages say that spare, uninstalled lithium batteries, including power banks, must be carried in carry-on baggage only. If a carry-on bag is checked at the gate, the spare batteries must be taken out and kept with the passenger in the cabin. Rechargeable lithium ion batteries are limited to 100 watt hours per battery. With airline approval, passengers may carry up to two spare larger batteries rated above 100 and up to 160 watt hours. Most batteries have no quantity limit as long as they are for the passenger's own use. Damaged or recalled batteries must not be carried unless they have been removed or otherwise made safe.

The FAA pages are not specific to drones. They tell you what to look for on the label of a drone battery: the watt-hour rating. If a pack is close to 100 watt hours, confirm its rating before you book, because the checkpoint is a bad place to find out.

## Protect the terminals

The FAA requires battery terminals to be protected from short circuit. Keep batteries in their retail packaging, tape the terminals, or use a battery case or a plastic bag for each battery. A battery case or an individual bag is also an easy way to keep loose metal in a backpack away from the contacts.

## Flights from Korea

Korea's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport announced a new in-flight safety standard on February 13, 2025, effective March 1, 2025, after a fire on an Air Busan aircraft. For power banks it says they may not be stored in overhead bins, may not be placed in checked baggage, may be carried in numbers of up to 5 at 100 watt hours or less and up to 2 at 100 to 160 watt hours with airline approval, and may not be charged from the aircraft's outlets. The announcement is about power banks, so ask your airline how it applies to drone batteries on your route, and look for newer notices because the rules may change.

## Heat and charge level on travel days

A closed car parked in the sun can become very hot, and high temperatures are bad for lithium batteries. Keep batteries with you rather than in a trunk or a locked car for hours. Manufacturers describe a recommended storage charge level for long storage in the battery's manual, and the DJI Mini 4 Pro manual notes that its intelligent flight batteries are put into hibernation mode before shipment for safety. Check your own battery's manual for the charge level it recommends before a long day of travel, and follow that rather than a rule of thumb from a forum.

## Protect the gimbal and the small parts

A padded case around the airframe is not enough if the gimbal and the camera can still move. Use your drone's gimbal protector or lock where the manufacturer provides one, keep the camera in its foam cutout and keep chargers, propellers and cables in their own compartments. DJI's Mini 4 Pro manual recommends fitting the gimbal protector and propeller holders when the aircraft is not in use. Check your own model's manual for the equivalent accessories.

## Pack spares for international trips

Do not assume you can buy your model's propellers or battery after you land. Pack a complete set of spare propellers, because a crash often damages more than one, one spare battery within the airline's limits, a charger with the right plug adapter and the correct tool for your model. Before you fly abroad, also read the local drone rules of your destination, for example our overview of [United States drone regulations](/en/regulations/us) and our guide to [Remote ID](/en/guides/remote-id-requirements-explained).

## Practical checklist

- Carry every spare battery in the cabin, never in checked baggage, with terminals protected.
- Confirm each battery's watt-hour rating and your airline's limits before booking.
- Keep batteries out of hot cars, and follow the storage advice in your manual for travel days.
- Lock or protect the gimbal and give the camera a dedicated slot.
- Bring a full spare propeller set, a spare battery, a charger adapter and the right tool.
- Check the rules of your departure and arrival countries and your airline's page before every trip.

## FAQ

### Can I put drone batteries in checked baggage?

The FAA says spare lithium batteries must be carried in carry-on baggage only. Airlines and national authorities can be stricter.

### What if my battery is above 100 watt hours?

The FAA allows up to two spare batteries of 101 to 160 watt hours with airline approval, and larger ones are not allowed. Ask the airline before you travel.

### Do the same rules apply everywhere?

No. The FAA says airlines may set stricter limits, and other countries have their own standards. The Korean example above applies to power banks and was announced in 2025.

### Does the drone itself need special handling?

The FAA pages say devices with batteries may be checked only if completely powered off and protected from accidental activation. Always check your airline's own policy first.

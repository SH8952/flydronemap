Switzerland is not an EU member, yet its drone rules are largely the EU's. The Federal Office of Civil Aviation (FOCA, in German BAZL) says that in Switzerland the same rules apply in principle as in the other EASA member states: the EU states, Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein. A pilot who knows the EU open category therefore already knows most of the Swiss rules. What is specifically Swiss is the registration process, the national drone map and the Daily Airspace Bulletin Switzerland (DABS), and FOCA expects pilots to consult them before every flight.

## Registration is for operators, not drones, and a camera makes it mandatory

According to FOCA, all private individuals and organisations that want to fly a drone in Switzerland must be registered as an operator. The only exception is a drone that weighs less than 250 g and has no device or sensor suited to recording personal data, such as a camera or a microphone. FOCA gives the example of a DJI Mini: it weighs less than 250 g, but because it has a camera, its operator must register. Drones themselves are not registered, because FOCA keeps a register of operators, not of drones.

Registration is done on FOCA's dLIS platform, and an eIAM account (AGOV or CH-Login) is needed to log in. Afterwards the operator receives a UAS operator number. It belongs to the person, so all your drones carry the same number. The first part of the number, CHE followed by 13 characters, must be clearly visible on each drone before every flight; failing to mark the drone is punishable. The last three characters after the hyphen are not applied to the drone. They are needed to activate Remote ID, and FOCA advises treating them like a password.

Operators must register in the EASA member state where they live or have their registered office, and once registered they may operate in any other EASA member state. An operator may not be registered in two EASA member states at the same time. Persons living outside the EASA member states must register in Switzerland unless they are already registered in another EASA member state. FOCA lists a minimum age of 12 for registration, with the name of a legal representative required for children under 16, and says the registration is valid for an unlimited period.

## Insurance, certificates and age depend on the weight of your drone

FOCA says operators must take out liability insurance with a guaranteed sum of at least 1 million francs before they operate drones of 250 g or more, and it recommends insuring lighter drones too. If you are not insured, you must expect a fine.

Remote pilots generally have to complete training and pass an exam. The exception is pilots who fly a class C0 drone, or a legacy or privately built drone under 250 g, in subcategory A1, for whom FOCA still recommends the training. The A1/A3 certificate is based on an online theory exam that can be taken from home. The A2 certificate requires the A1/A3 certificate first, a practical self-study, and a theory exam taken on site. Certificates are valid for five years in all EASA member states, and you must be able to show yours during flights, on paper or digitally. Certificates issued in EU states, Norway or Iceland are valid in Switzerland, while certificates from states outside EASA are not recognised.

In the open category the minimum age is 12. Younger children may fly only under the direct supervision of a person aged at least 16 who has the required competence. In the special category the minimum age is 14. FOCA notes that the minimum age can differ from country to country, so check it before flying abroad. For current fees and exam details, use the FOCA pages.

## General rules come first: 120 m, line of sight, no crowds and Remote ID on

FOCA's flight rules for the open category apply in every subcategory:

- The maximum height is 120 m above ground.
- Flying over crowds is prohibited. FOCA defines a crowd as so many people standing so close together that it is almost impossible for one person to leave, such as events, ski slopes, full shopping streets, and busy parks and beaches.
- You may only fly within your own line of sight (VLOS). Night flights within sight are allowed if the drone has position lights, but the distance must then be shortened. Flights beyond visual line of sight are prohibited in the open category.
- Manned aviation always has priority, and you must land if blue-light services operate nearby.
- Remote ID must be switched on during the flight, using your operator number. Class C0 drones, legacy drones and privately built drones are exempt from this rule.
- With video goggles (FPV), an observer must stand next to you and keep the drone in direct sight. Without one, the flight counts as beyond visual line of sight and belongs in the special category.

Flights that cannot follow these rules need a FOCA authorisation in the special category. FOCA lists examples such as drones over 25 kg, flights above 120 m, flights beyond visual line of sight, flights over crowds and flights with dangerous goods.

## A1, A2 and A3 differ in distance to uninvolved people, and A2 and A3 add a 1:1 rule

- A1 covers class C0 drones and legacy or privately built drones under 250 g. Overflight of uninvolved people is allowed. With class C1, overflight should be avoided, and if it happens anyway the drone must be steered away from the people immediately.
- A2 prohibits overflight of uninvolved people. The horizontal distance is the greater of two values: at least 30 m (5 m in low-speed mode), or a distance equal to the flight height (the 1:1 rule). FOCA's example: at 40 m height the distance is at least 40 m, while at 20 m height it is still at least 30 m.
- A3 also prohibits overflight. You must stay at least 150 m horizontally from residential, commercial, industrial and recreational areas. In addition, the distance to uninvolved people is the greater of 30 m and the flight height. FOCA explains that the remote pilot must assess the area on site, and it describes the 150 m rule as applying to areas where 10 or more uninvolved people are within a 100 m radius at the time of the flight.

FOCA adds that the open-category rules contain no explicit provisions for towns and agglomerations. Whether a flight there is possible must be judged case by case, and the ban on flying over crowds and the distance to uninvolved people are decisive.

## The drone map and the DABS are both mandatory checks, and the permit comes from the zone's own authority

FOCA says there are areas in Switzerland where flying drones is prohibited or only possible with restrictions, and that pilots are obliged to inform themselves before every flight. The interactive drone map shows national and cantonal restriction areas. Its content is constantly changing, so it must be consulted before each flight. FOCA lists the types of restriction: control zones of airfields (CTR), airfield perimeters, correctional facilities, wildlife protection areas, nuclear power plants, military areas, energy and gas supply infrastructure, and additional restrictions at cantonal level.

FOCA describes three steps. First, open the drone map, click on your intended flight area and check whether a restriction applies to you. If it does, you need a permit from the competent body shown in the additional information, not from FOCA. Second, check the latest DABS, which shows the most important notices (NOTAMs) of civil aviation on a map and is updated several times a day. If a restriction in the DABS affects your flight, the flight must not take place. Third, check the areas with an increased collision risk: hospital helipads, mountain landing sites, danger zones of army shooting ranges, and paragliding, hang-gliding and gliding areas. Adapt your flight, and land if a helicopter approaches.

Several areas can overlap on the same point, and each has its own rule and contact, so read every entry. FlyDroneMap's [home map](/en) also displays this layer for reference, but confirm on the official map and in the DABS before a flight. FOCA names the VLK (SR 748.941), the Swiss ordinance on aircraft of special categories, among the legal bases for these restrictions.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Assuming that an EASA registration or certificate means there is nothing left to check in Switzerland, although the geographical restrictions and the DABS apply to everyone.
- Checking the drone map but not the DABS, or the other way round.
- Reading only the first zone at a location, when several zones can overlap there.
- Flying in a town without assessing the crowd ban and the distances, because the open-category rules say nothing explicit about agglomerations.
- Forgetting the operator number on the drone, or switching Remote ID on without entering the operator number.

## Practical checklist

- Register as an operator on dLIS unless your drone is under 250 g and has no camera, microphone or other sensor for personal data, and mark every drone with the visible part of your operator number.
- If your drone weighs 250 g or more, check that your liability insurance meets the minimum FOCA states.
- Make sure you hold the certificate your flight requires (A1/A3 or A2), meet the minimum age and can show the certificate.
- Before every flight, check the drone map, the current DABS and the areas with an increased collision risk, and request a permit from the competent body if a restriction applies.
- Plan your distances for the subcategory, stay within line of sight and below 120 m, and switch Remote ID on.
- Always check current requirements through the official channels of FOCA. This guide is orientation only and does not replace a flight permit or legal advice.

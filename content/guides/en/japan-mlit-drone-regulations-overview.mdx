Pilots arriving in Japan from the US, Korea, or most of Europe carry one assumption that gets them in trouble fast: that 250 grams is the line for whether a drone needs to be registered. Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) set its own threshold well below that, and registration is only one of three separate systems — registration, a risk-based flight category, and location-specific approval — that a flight can trigger independently of each other. Here's how they actually fit together.

## Japan's threshold is 100 grams, not 250

Under Japan's Civil Aeronautics Act, any drone with a takeoff weight of 100g or more — battery included — must be registered through DIPS (the Drone/UAS Information Platform System) before it flies, and unregistered flight of a qualifying drone is a violation with real penalties attached. The registration itself is valid for three years and needs renewing. The trap is specific: a "toy-class" drone that ships under 250g and needs no paperwork in the US or Korea can still clear Japan's 100g bar with room to spare, so pilots who check a spec sheet against the threshold they learned elsewhere end up flying unregistered without realizing it. Registered drones also broadcast Remote ID by default, with only a narrow exemption for aircraft registered before the mandate and flown within specific pre-notified areas.

## Three risk categories decide whether you need permission at all

MLIT sorts flights into three categories by risk rather than treating every flight the same way. Category I needs no MLIT approval — daytime, visual line of sight, away from Densely Inhabited Districts, airports, and people not involved in the flight, under 150m altitude. Category II covers flights that cross into one of those conditions but keep a safety buffer (an observer, a controlled perimeter), and requires MLIT approval with a documented safety plan. Category III is flights beyond visual line of sight over people with no safety measures at all, and demands a certified pilot plus a much heavier risk assessment. In practice, a hobby drone flown over an empty rice field far from any town needs no MLIT paperwork whatsoever, while the same drone launched from a city park can require full Category II approval — even though the park itself looks just as quiet as the field.

## DID coverage is about population density, not what the ground looks like

That city-park example matters because a Densely Inhabited District (DID) is a statistical boundary based on population density across a wider area, not a judgment call about how many people happen to be standing nearby at that moment. DIDs cover roughly 4% of Japan's land area but around 70% of its population, which means most of the convenient, easy-access flying spots near where people actually live default to DID status. The mistake this produces is predictable: a pilot flies at 6am in a nearly empty neighborhood park, assumes the emptiness means no restriction applies, and never checks the DID map — when in fact the surrounding district's density is what triggers the requirement, independent of who's actually there right now.

## Pilot certification is only mandatory for a subset of flights

Japan's unmanned aircraft pilot skill certification comes in two tiers (1st class and 2nd class), but it is not a blanket requirement just because a flight needs MLIT approval. Most Category II flights are cleared through documented safety measures and a manual, not a certified pilot — certification is specifically tied to the highest-risk Category III operations, like BVLOS flight over people with no observer. This cuts both ways in practice: some pilots over-invest in certification for a routine DID overflight that only needed a Category II application, while others assume a simple DIPS registration is enough to cover an actual BVLOS-over-people job and find out otherwise when the application is rejected.

## Other rules that apply no matter what

A separate law protecting important facilities — national government buildings, the Imperial Palace, nuclear power plants, and defense-related sites among them — can prohibit flight nearby regardless of the drone's weight, registration status, or flight category, so this isn't something registration or a Category I flight plan clears you of. The Civil Aeronautics Act also sets baseline operating rules for every flight: no flying under the influence of alcohol, a pre-flight inspection, roughly 30m of separation from people and property not involved in the flight, no flying over crowds or events, no dropping objects, and a general 150m altitude ceiling outside specifically approved zones.

## Common mistakes worth avoiding

- Assuming the 250g threshold used in the US or Korea applies in Japan, and skipping DIPS registration for a drone that actually clears Japan's 100g line.
- Judging DID status by how quiet a specific spot looks rather than checking the DID boundary against the site.
- Believing MLIT approval always means a certified pilot is required, when most Category II flights don't need one.
- Overlooking the separate important-facilities prohibition, which can apply even to a registered, properly permitted drone.

## Practical checklist

- Weigh your drone with the battery installed and register it through DIPS if it's 100g or more.
- Check whether your planned site falls inside a DID before assuming no approval is needed.
- Work out which of the three flight categories your plan falls into, and file for Category II or III approval accordingly.
- Confirm whether pilot certification actually applies to your specific flight rather than assuming it always — or never — does.
- Always verify current requirements and get final approval through the official DIPS portal — this guide is reference information only and does not substitute for an actual flight approval.

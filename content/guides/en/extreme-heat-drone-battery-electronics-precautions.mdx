Cold weather gets most of the attention in drone forums, but heat causes a different and in some ways more serious set of failures — from batteries that swell inside a hot car to onboard electronics that throttle themselves mid-flight. Understanding why heat behaves so differently from cold changes how you prepare for a summer flight.

## Why LiPo batteries degrade faster in heat than pilots expect

Lithium-polymer chemistry starts breaking down internally once the cell climbs past roughly 40-45°C (104-113°F) sustained, well below what a battery can reach just sitting on black plastic in direct sun. A drone case left on asphalt or a car dashboard on a 30°C (86°F) day can trap enough solar heat to push the battery inside past that threshold before the aircraft ever leaves the ground — the surrounding air temperature alone understates the real risk, because direct sunlight and a dark surface do most of the heating. A battery that feels warm to the touch, looks slightly puffy, or has a case that no longer sits flush has already suffered internal gas buildup from electrolyte breakdown, and should be retired rather than flown; a swollen LiPo is a fire risk, not just a performance issue.

## Onboard electronics can throttle or shut down mid-flight

Flight controllers and electronic speed controllers (ESCs) carry their own thermal limits, and many consumer drones will display an overheating warning, throttle motor output, or force a landing once internal temperatures climb too high. Hovering for a photo or video shoot is actually worse for heat buildup than forward flight, because forward motion pushes cooling air across the electronics while a stationary hover in still, hot midday air relies on much weaker natural convection. Pilots doing long static hover shoots — real estate listings, inspection holds — in direct summer sun are the ones most likely to see an unexpected overheat warning appear with no wind or battery-related trigger at all.

## Heat shows up in the footage, not just the flight log

A camera sensor that has been sitting in direct sun for several minutes before takeoff runs hotter than normal, which increases image noise and can introduce visible hot pixels or banding in video, especially during long, static shots. Gimbals can also behave slightly stiffer or hunt more when their motors are already warm before the flight even starts. Letting the aircraft sit in shade for a few minutes immediately before a long or technically demanding shot is a simple step that measurably improves footage quality on hot days.

## The hot-car mistake pilots don't think about

Leaving a drone case or spare batteries inside a parked car is one of the most common ways pilots unknowingly start a flight already partway toward an unsafe temperature. Car interiors in direct sun are well documented to exceed 60°C (140°F) within about an hour even when the outside air is only around 30°C (86°F) — far hotter than the air temperature alone would suggest, and hot enough to meaningfully pre-heat a battery before it's even removed from the case. A battery that starts a flight already warm has far less thermal headroom left before it crosses into the danger zone described above.

## Charging and storage after a hot flight

Charging a battery immediately after a hot flight, before it has cooled back down, adds electrical stress on top of thermal stress and is a commonly cited contributor to LiPo fires. Let a battery cool to close to room temperature — most manufacturers suggest waiting until it's no longer warm to the touch — before connecting it to a charger, and never charge inside a hot car or in direct sun. Store spare batteries in a shaded, insulated bag rather than an equipment case left in the sun between flights.

## Practical checklist

- Never fly a battery that feels warm at rest, looks puffy, or has a case that no longer sits flat — retire it instead.
- On hot, sunny days, keep batteries and the aircraft in shade until just before takeoff rather than leaving them on a dashboard or in a hot case.
- Expect overheat warnings to be more likely during long static hovers in direct midday sun than during forward flight; build in short breaks for long shoots.
- Let the camera and gimbal sit in shade for a few minutes before a long or technically demanding shot on a hot day.
- Let a battery cool to near room temperature before charging, and never charge in a parked car or direct sunlight.

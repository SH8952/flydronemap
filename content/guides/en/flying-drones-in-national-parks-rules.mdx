This guide covers the United States. A visitor who checks only the FAA's tools before a sunrise shoot can see no airspace restriction and still be breaking a federal rule, because the National Park Service is a different agency with a different rule. The two systems answer different questions, and satisfying one does not satisfy the other.

## Key takeaways

- NPS Policy Memorandum 14-05 (June 2014) directs superintendents to use their authority under 36 CFR 1.5 to prohibit the launching, landing or operation of unmanned aircraft on lands and waters administered by the National Park Service.
- The NPS says the memorandum is still in force with a few exceptions, and that violating the ban is a misdemeanor with a maximum penalty of six months in jail and a 5,000 dollar fine.
- The memorandum does not change any FAA requirement, so FAA rules and NPS rules both apply.
- NPS jurisdiction ends at the park boundary. The NPS page does not discuss launching from outside a park, so ask the park if your plan depends on it.
- If you want to fly in a park, ask the park superintendent whether a designated area or a special use permit is possible.

## The rule and where it comes from

In June 2014 the NPS issued Policy Memorandum 14-05. It tells each superintendent to use the authority under 36 CFR 1.5 to prohibit launching, landing or operating unmanned aircraft on NPS-administered lands and waters, subject to the memorandum's conditions and exceptions. The NPS describes the action as covering the launching, landing and operation of uncrewed aircraft on those lands and waters. Other regulations can apply to drone use as well: 36 CFR 2.2 deals with wildlife harassment, 36 CFR 2.34 with disorderly conduct, and 36 CFR 2.12(a)(3) with portable motors in non-developed areas unless a permit is held.

## Why FAA clearance does not help you here

A Part 107 certificate, a clean airspace check and a LAANC authorization all address airspace. The NPS rule addresses the land and water the park manages. The NPS says the memorandum does not modify any requirement imposed by the FAA, which means a pilot has to satisfy both. A commercial pilot with an airspace authorization is not exempt from the park rule, and neither is a hobby pilot. If you want to review the FAA side, see our [Part 107 overview](/en/guides/faa-part-107-explained) and our guide to [airspace classes](/en/guides/understanding-us-airspace-classes).

## What counts as operating in a park

The NPS wording is about launching, landing and operating on park lands and waters. It also says its jurisdiction ends at the park boundary, and it does not address launching from outside a park. Because that leaves room for doubt, ask the park before you rely on an outside launch point, keep the drone clear of park land and water, and do not assume that a nearby site is allowed without checking who manages it. State parks and other public lands are governed by their own rules, so a nearby site tells you nothing about a national park.

## The stated penalty

The NPS states that violating the ban is a misdemeanor with the maximum penalty of six months in jail and a 5,000 dollar fine. Rangers enforce the prohibition and have discretion to handle potential violations case by case. Other charges listed in the regulations above can be added in a given situation.

## Permits and exceptions

For recreational flying, the NPS tells people to check the park's website to see whether it is allowed. If it is not, ask the park superintendent whether an area can be designated within the park or whether a special use permit can be obtained. The NPS itself uses uncrewed aircraft for purposes such as search and rescue, fire operations, scientific study and aerial photography when approved by the superintendent. Contact the park well before a trip, not at the entrance gate.

## Practical checklist

- Treat NPS land and water as closed to drone launching, landing and operation unless the park confirms otherwise.
- Do not rely on an airspace app or an authorization alone, because they do not cover NPS land-use rules.
- Check the park's own website and contact the superintendent's office before you plan a shoot.
- If you consider an outside launch point, ask the park first and keep the whole flight away from park land and water.
- Keep a record of what the park told you.
- Confirm current rules with the NPS and the FAA. This page is a planning reference, not legal advice.

## FAQ

### Is the ban the same in every national park?

The memorandum directs superintendents to apply the prohibition and describes exceptions. Individual parks can publish their own pages, so read the page of the park you plan to visit.

### Can I fly if I have a Part 107 certificate?

The certificate and an airspace authorization do not change the NPS rule. The NPS says its memorandum does not modify FAA requirements, so you need to meet both.

### Is there a way to get permission?

The NPS suggests asking the superintendent whether an area can be designated or a special use permit obtained. It does not promise either.

### What is the penalty?

According to the NPS, a misdemeanor with a maximum of six months in jail and a 5,000 dollar fine.

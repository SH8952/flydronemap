Imagine flying a survey mission in a low-latitude country an hour after sunset, and your drone's GPS starts losing lock every few minutes even though the Kp index for the day never rose above 3. That could be ionospheric scintillation: a rapid, small-scale disturbance that behaves nothing like the broad, slow geomagnetic storms most drone weather guidance is built around.

## Key takeaways

- Scintillation is rapid fluctuation in the amplitude and phase of a radio signal, caused by small-scale structure in the ionosphere's electron density.
- It is most frequent at low and high latitudes. NOAA says the equatorial form is most severe just after sunset and is part of the natural day-night cycle of the equatorial ionosphere, not a storm effect.
- A white paper by the SBAS Ionospheric Working Group says GPS scintillation generally occurs shortly after sunset and may persist until just after local midnight, most severe in equatorial anomaly regions roughly 15 degrees north and south of the magnetic equator.
- Severe scintillation can prevent a receiver from locking on and make position calculation impossible; milder cases reduce accuracy and confidence.
- A low Kp index does not rule it out. Plan for it using location and local time, and have a backup window or a manual-flying plan.

## What scintillation actually is

NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center defines ionospheric scintillation as the rapid modification of radio waves caused by small-scale structures in the ionosphere, with structure sizes from tens of meters to tens of kilometers in the electron density. NOAA compares the effect to waves on the surface of water scattering light. Two indexes describe it: S4 for amplitude scintillation and sigma-phi for phase scintillation, both reflecting how much the signal varies over a period of time, usually one minute.

When the amplitude fades are deep, signal levels can drop below a receiver's lock threshold. Rapid phase changes can also cause cycle slips, which matter mostly for high-precision carrier-phase positioning. The SBAS white paper adds that interrupted carrier-phase smoothing can increase ranging errors several times over, though how this appears on a consumer drone depends on the receiver.

## Where and when it happens

| Region | Typical timing | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Near the magnetic equator, roughly 15 degrees north and south | Shortly after local sunset, sometimes until just after local midnight | Natural day-night cycle of the equatorial ionosphere (NOAA) |
| High latitudes, including auroral regions | Can occur in all seasons | Accompanies aurora and geomagnetic activity (SBAS white paper) |
| Mid-latitudes such as the United States | Much less often | Mainly extreme magnetic storms |

The white paper also notes that activity follows the 11-year solar cycle and is stronger around solar maximum. NOAA lists local time, season, geomagnetic activity and solar cycle as influences.

## Why a low Kp index does not make you safe

Equatorial scintillation follows the local-time structure of the ionosphere at dusk, not global geomagnetic activity. NOAA states that it is not associated with any space weather storm, so it can be present on a night when the Kp index sits at 1 or 2, a reading most pilots would read as calm. NOAA also notes that near the equator dual-frequency GPS receivers often lose lock because of scintillation. At high latitudes, scintillation does accompany geomagnetic activity, so aurora-season advice applies; see the guide on [aurora season and high-latitude navigation anomalies](/en/guides/aurora-season-and-high-latitude-drone-navigation-anomalies) for that case.

Scintillation is separate from the general ionospheric delay that storms cause. NOAA explains that in calm conditions single-frequency GPS systems can provide position accuracy of a meter or less, while during a severe space weather storm those errors can increase to tens of meters or more. That slow, broad error is what the Kp-based guidance in [what the Kp index is](/en/guides/what-is-the-kp-index) is about.

## What it can look like on a drone

Your GPS confidence display, satellite count or HDOP can look fine one moment and then briefly degrade or drop a fix the next, without any obvious external cause. The aircraft may hover less steadily or switch into a mode that does not use satellite positioning. The exact reaction depends on the manufacturer and model; DJI's Mini 4K manual, for example, says the aircraft automatically changes to ATTI mode when the GPS signal is weak, and in that mode wind can cause horizontal shifting.

## Common mistakes

- Treating scintillation as a hardware problem, such as a faulty GPS module, loose antenna or motor interference, because intermittent lock loss looks identical to a receiver fault. Check whether the timing and location fit scintillation before replacing anything.
- Scheduling precision work in the post-sunset window in equatorial regions purely because the day's heat or wind has eased.
- Power-cycling the drone repeatedly to chase a bad fix, when the disturbance may pass later in the night.
- Assuming a quiet Kp index guarantees good GPS everywhere on Earth.

## Practical checklist

- If you fly regularly in low-latitude regions near the magnetic equator, treat the hours from sunset to around local midnight as a higher-risk window for GPS lock problems, independent of the day's Kp index.
- At high latitudes, treat active aurora and geomagnetic storm periods as an extra risk window; see how solar flares affect drone navigation.
- Keep the flight within sight and be ready to take manual control if the lock drops. The steps are in [what to do when GPS signal is weak](/en/guides/what-to-do-when-drone-gps-signal-is-weak).
- For high-precision missions in scintillation-prone regions, schedule a backup window in daytime hours if the job allows it.
- Check the [FlyDroneMap dashboard](/en) for the current space-weather picture, remembering that it does not predict equatorial scintillation.

## FAQ

### Is scintillation caused by solar storms?

Not at the equator. NOAA states that equatorial scintillation is part of the natural day-night cycle of the ionosphere and is not associated with any space weather storm. At high latitudes it does accompany aurora and geomagnetic activity.

### Does the Kp index tell me if scintillation is happening?

No. Kp describes global geomagnetic activity, while equatorial scintillation follows local time and location. A low Kp does not rule it out.

### How long does it last?

The SBAS white paper says it generally occurs shortly after sunset and may persist until just after local midnight in the equatorial regions. Individual events vary, so use this as a rough pattern, not a guarantee.

### Should I stop flying?

That is your decision as pilot in command. If your drone reports GPS trouble, follow your manual, land safely, and consider flying in daylight hours or another day. Do not take off with a GPS warning on the display.

VLOS (Visual Line of Sight) means flying a drone only within a range where the pilot (or an observer) can see it continuously with the naked eye, without aids like binoculars or a screen. BVLOS (Beyond Visual Line of Sight) means the drone flies outside that range, relying on a camera feed or autonomous navigation instead.

## Why the distinction matters

In most countries, standard hobbyist and commercial drone rules are built around VLOS as the default. The FAA's Part 107 in the US, and similar frameworks elsewhere, assume the pilot can visually spot and avoid nearby manned aircraft and obstacles at all times.

## Why BVLOS gets special treatment

Once a drone is out of sight, the pilot can't visually detect and avoid hazards, so BVLOS flights typically require a separate waiver or authorization, plus extra equipment like detect-and-avoid systems. Much of the drone industry's growth — package delivery, long-distance infrastructure inspection — hinges on relaxed BVLOS rules, which is why aviation authorities worldwide are actively updating BVLOS regulations.

## What pilots should know

- Without special authorization, ordinary pilots are generally limited to flying within VLOS.
- Fog, nighttime, or a drone passing behind a building — anything where you can see it on-screen but not with your own eyes — can put you in BVLOS territory without realizing it.
- Using a visual observer to maintain eyes on the drone is an accepted way to stay VLOS-compliant under some rules.

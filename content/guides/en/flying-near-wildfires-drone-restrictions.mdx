This guide covers the United States. The FAA often sets up a temporary flight restriction, or TFR, around a wildfire, and all aircraft, drones included, are barred from the restricted area unless an agency involved in the firefighting operation operates them. A photographer who sees dramatic smoke has no way to know from the ground what is already in the air above it.

## Key takeaways

- Wildfire TFRs are published as NOTAMs. Section 91.137 of 14 CFR is the part of the regulations used for disaster and hazard areas, and it allows only aircraft taking part in the response to enter, with a few listed exceptions that do not cover ordinary drone flights.
- Fire response agencies often ground their aircraft to avoid the potential for a midair collision, and a drone can lead fire managers to suspend aerial suppression.
- The FAA says interfering with firefighting efforts on public lands is a federal crime punishable by up to 12 months in prison, and that interfering with firefighting aircraft is a crime even without a TFR.
- Civil penalties exist as well. 49 U.S.C. 46320 sets a maximum of 20,000 dollars, and the FAA has stated in 2025 that it can impose civil penalties of up to 75,000 dollars in connection with wildfire suppression or emergency response when TFRs are in place.
- The FAA advises staying away from wildfires even when no TFR exists.

## How a wildfire TFR works

Section 91.137 allows the FAA to issue a NOTAM for an area when needed to protect people and property from a surface hazard, to give disaster relief aircraft a safe environment, or to prevent unsafe congestion of sightseeing and other aircraft over an incident of high public interest. The text does not mention wildfires by name, and it sets no distances; each NOTAM sets its own area. The FAA's pilot guide says these restrictions may last a few hours or several days, and that their boundaries follow the needs of the agency coordinating the response, so they are not always circular. Because a fire can change quickly, a check made in the morning says little about the afternoon.

## Why one drone can pause the response

The FAA's wildfire toolkit says fire response agencies often ground their aircraft to avoid the potential for a midair collision. Unauthorized drone flights can lead fire managers to suspend aerial suppression such as airtankers and helicopters until the drone has left and they are confident it will not return. The toolkit's slogan is "If You Fly, Firefighters Can't." That is the practical effect: the people on the ground who depend on aircraft support lose it while a hobby flight is under way. Our [TFR guide](/en/guides/temporary-flight-restrictions-tfr-explained) explains the wider system.

## A documented example

In January 2025 a water-dropping firefighting aircraft was grounded after a civilian drone reportedly left a football-sized hole in its wing during the Palisades Fire in Southern California. The aircraft landed safely, and the FAA said it was investigating. This is a news report of one event and not a prediction for your flight, but it shows why agencies take these flights seriously.

## Penalties

The FAA's toolkit says interfering with firefighting efforts on public lands is a federal crime punishable by up to 12 months in prison, and that Congress authorized the FAA to impose a civil penalty of up to 20,000 dollars on a drone pilot who interferes with wildfire suppression, law enforcement or emergency response. The statute behind that figure, 49 U.S.C. 46320, applies to a person who knowingly or recklessly interferes with such an effort. In January 2025 the FAA also said that it can impose civil penalties of up to 75,000 dollars on drone pilots who interfere with wildfire suppression or emergency response when TFRs are in place. The two figures come from different statements and legal bases, so the amount that applies depends on the case. Penalty limits can change, so confirm the current figures with the FAA.

## No TFR does not mean no problem

The FAA toolkit states that interfering with firefighting aircraft is a federal crime even when no TFR is in place, and advises pilots to avoid flying near wildfires even without a TFR. A Part 107 certificate, a LAANC authorization or a recreational pilot's safety test does not give you permission to enter a wildfire TFR; only the agency running the air operations can allow that. See our guides to [Part 107](/en/guides/faa-part-107-explained) and to [LAANC](/en/guides/what-is-laanc-and-why-it-matters) for what those approvals do cover.

## Practical checklist

- Before any flight near smoke or a reported fire, check tfr.faa.gov and a current NOTAM briefing immediately before launch.
- Assume the area is restricted even if no TFR has appeared yet.
- Do not use distance or altitude from the flames as a safety margin.
- If aircraft arrive or a TFR is announced while you are flying, land at once.
- Confirm current rules and penalty figures with the FAA. This site's layers are planning references and not a live TFR feed.

## FAQ

### Can I fly a drone to film a wildfire?

Not inside the restricted area. The FAA says all aircraft, including drones, are barred unless an agency involved in the firefighting operation operates them, and advises avoiding wildfires even when no TFR exists.

### Who can fly drones there?

Agencies involved in the firefighting operation, under the direction of the officials running the air operations.

### What are the penalties?

The FAA cites up to 12 months in prison for interfering with firefighting efforts on public lands and civil penalties; the figures it has cited are 20,000 dollars in its toolkit and statute, and up to 75,000 dollars in a January 2025 statement.

### What if I did not know about the TFR?

Check before every flight, because TFRs can appear on short notice. Land at once if you learn of one while flying.
